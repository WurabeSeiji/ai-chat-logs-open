\subsubsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Against the background of the framework of complex Hilbert spaces,
observables, and measurement developed in Chapters 1 to 3 of Akira
Shimizu's \emph{New Edition: Foundations of Quantum Theory} {[}1{]},
this paper organizes five stepwise thought experiments to unify the
treatment of measurement precision, the uncertainty relation, wave
packets, quantum correlations, and the algebra of observables. The
starting point is the ``identification wall'' of classical measurement
theory, and the destination is the algebraic picture in which ``physical
quantities satisfying an uncertainty relation are different projections
extracted from the same wave packet.'' The core reading of the present
paper is a semiclassical and geometric interpretation that positions the
invariants in phase space as symplectic capacities, exemplified by de
Gosson's quantum blob {[}9{]}. The present paper does not contradict the
mathematical system of standard quantum theory; rather, it offers one
reading that supplements its conceptual outlook.

\subsection{Thought Experiment I: The Identification Wall in
Measurement}\label{thought-experiment-i-the-identification-wall-in-measurement}

\subsubsection{Setting}\label{setting}

Suppose a physical quantity \emph{A} takes real values. We measure the
distance \emph{L} between two points \emph{A}₁ and \emph{A}₂ using a
measure based on the concept of distance, which returns only discrete
values with a minimum spacing of Δ.

At each measurement, one of the two adjacent lattice points \{\emph{k}Δ,
(\emph{k}+1)Δ\} bracketing the true value is returned with probability
depending on the position \emph{r} of the true value within the cell:

P(up) = \emph{r}/Δ, P(down) = 1 − \emph{r}/Δ

(position-dependent dithering).

\subsubsection{Behavior of the N-fold
Average}\label{behavior-of-the-n-fold-average}

When \emph{A}₁ = \emph{A}₂, the single-shot distance \emph{D} = ε₂ − ε₁
takes values \{−Δ, 0, +Δ\}. Writing the within-cell position of the true
value as \emph{q} = \emph{r}/Δ ∈ {[}0, 1{]}, in general:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  P(\emph{D} = +Δ) = \emph{q}(1−\emph{q})
\item
  P(\emph{D} = −Δ) = \emph{q}(1−\emph{q})
\item
  P(\emph{D} = 0) = \emph{q}² + (1−\emph{q})²
\end{itemize}

Hence E{[}\emph{D}{]} = 0, Var{[}\emph{D}{]} = 2\emph{q}(1−\emph{q})Δ²,
σ\_\emph{D} = √(2\emph{q}(1−\emph{q}))·Δ.

In particular, at the symmetric position \emph{q} = 1/2, the probability
distribution is \{1/4, 1/2, 1/4\}, Var{[}\emph{D}{]} = Δ²/2, and σ\_N =
Δ/√(2N).

As N → ∞, σ\_N → 0, and L̂\_N → \emph{L} with probability 1.

\subsubsection{Condition for
Distinguishability}\label{condition-for-distinguishability}

For \emph{A}₁ and \emph{A}₂ to be recognized as two distinct objects,
the two must belong to different Δ cells. If they fall within the same
cell, the measure cannot distinguish ``one point or two,'' and the
quantity ``distance between two points'' cannot be constituted.

Therefore the condition for the problem of measuring \emph{L} to be
well-posed is

\emph{L} ≥ Δ.

The region \emph{L} \textless{} Δ is not ``buried in measurement
error''; it is a region in which the very concept of ``two points'' is
not defined.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

\begin{quote}
When a measure based on distance is used to measure distance,
measurement at scales below the measure's spacing Δ is
\textbf{undefined} prior to any invocation of the uncertainty principle.
Δ functions not as a resolution but as the threshold of individuation.

Within the range \emph{L} ≥ Δ, arbitrary precision is attainable by
repeated measurement regardless of the absolute value of Δ.
\end{quote}

The present argument holds in general for any measure based on the
concept of distance and has a structural analogy with, but is positioned
independently of, the discussions of minimum length at the Planck scale
in quantum gravity {[}10{]}.

\textbf{More rigorously}: rather than the ontological statement ``no two
points exist below Δ,'' the appropriate formulation is ``no projection
operator distinguishing two points exists in the observable algebra
generated by a Δ-step measure.'' The present discussion can be made
consistent with the framework of POVM and Kraus operators in generalized
measurement theory, where it appears as a special case of coarse-grained
measurement.

Note also that when \emph{A}₁ and \emph{A}₂ are externally pre-labeled
as distinct objects, the mean sub-cell difference can be estimated by
repeated measurements (the \emph{N} → ∞ convergence via
position-dependent dithering). The ``undefinability'' referred to in the
present paper applies to the case where no operation exists within the
observing system itself to individuate two objects.

\subsection{Thought Experiment II: The Locus of
Fluctuation}\label{thought-experiment-ii-the-locus-of-fluctuation}

\subsubsection{Setting}\label{setting-1}

Consider the reverse situation. The measure has infinite precision and
returns its input value unchanged. The physical quantity \emph{A}
itself, however, fluctuates about its true value \emph{A'} according to

\emph{A}\_i = \emph{A'} + ξ\_i, ξ\_i \textasciitilde{} Uniform{[}−Δ/2,
+Δ/2{]}.

We assume \emph{L} \textgreater{} Δ.

\subsubsection{N-fold Average}\label{n-fold-average}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  E{[}L̂\_N{]} = \emph{A'}
\item
  Var{[}\emph{L}\_i{]} = Δ²/12
\item
  σ\_N = Δ/√(12N) → 0
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Comparison with Thought Experiment
I}\label{comparison-with-thought-experiment-i}

Instantiating both cases with true value 7.7 and spacing Δ = 1, each
yields the same empirical distribution: out of 100 measurements, roughly
30 return 7 and 70 return 8, with the average converging to 7.7.

\subsubsection{Two-sided Fluctuation}\label{two-sided-fluctuation}

When fluctuations ξ (width δ) on the quantity side and η (width σ) on
the measure side coexist independently:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Observed value: \emph{L}\_i = \emph{A'} + ξ\_i + η\_i
\item
  σ\_N = √(Var{[}ξ{]} + Var{[}η{]}) / √N → 0
\end{itemize}

The 1/√N decay persists, but separating δ and σ from a single observed
series requires additional assumptions external to the observation
(independent calibration of the measure, time-series decomposition,
prior knowledge of distribution shapes, etc.).

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-1}

\begin{quote}
Defining the observed fluctuation magnitude as Δ\_obs, one cannot
determine from observed data alone whether it originates from the
quantization width of the measure, from the fluctuation of the quantity
itself, or from a combination of both.

Rather than ``convergence to the objective true value,'' the precise
statement is ``convergence to the effective true value defined by the
observer,'' and the 1/√N decay applies rigorously in this sense.
\end{quote}

\textbf{More rigorously}: ``cannot be determined from observed data
alone'' means ``under the condition of a single observed series, a
single measurement setup, and no prior calibration.'' If the apparatus
Hamiltonian is known, system-side and environment(measure)-side
fluctuations can in principle be distinguished via open-system
decoherence theory. The present paper's claim is unidentifiability from
observation alone, which connects to the observer-knowledge-dependent
epistemological aspect (QBism-type interpretations).

\subsection{Thought Experiment III: Wavelength Representation of
Momentum and the Uncertainty
Relation}\label{thought-experiment-iii-wavelength-representation-of-momentum-and-the-uncertainty-relation}

\subsubsection{Setting}\label{setting-2}

We extend the object of Thought Experiment II to a quantum-theoretic
wave packet. For position \emph{x}, consider the center position
(expectation value ⟨\emph{x}⟩) \emph{x}₁ and the spread δ\emph{x} of the
wave packet. Here ``center position'' is merely a label of the packet
distribution; we do not take the position that there exists an objective
true value external to observation.

Similarly for momentum \emph{p}, consider the center position
(expectation value ⟨\emph{p}⟩) \emph{p}₁ and the spread δ\emph{p}.

\subsubsection{Rewriting via Wavelength}\label{rewriting-via-wavelength}

From the de Broglie relation {[}2{]}:

\[p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k, \quad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\]

the momentum fluctuation is

\[|\delta p| = \frac{h}{\lambda^2}|\delta\lambda| = \hbar \, |\delta k|.\]

(The differential relation d\emph{p}/dλ = −\emph{h}/λ² has a sign, but
we take absolute values for fluctuation magnitudes.)

Rewriting the uncertainty relation Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{p} ≥ ℏ/2 {[}7{]} in
terms of the wavenumber:

\[\Delta x \cdot \Delta k \geq \frac{1}{2}.\]

\subsubsection{Observation}\label{observation}

In this form, Planck's constant ℏ disappears from both sides. What
remains is a mathematical inequality of Fourier transforms concerning
the product of position and wavenumber.

Planck's constant ℏ functions as a dimensional conversion factor
connecting the unit of position (m) and the unit of momentum (kg·m/s). A
similar view that treats Planck's constant not as an observable quantity
but as a convention of human unit-choice has been proposed by Ralston
{[}8{]}.

Qualitatively:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Pinning position precisely ⟺ narrowing the position wave packet ⟺
  broadening the wavelength distribution ⟺ making momentum vague
\item
  Pinning momentum precisely ⟺ narrowing the wavelength to one ⟺
  spreading over all of space ⟺ making position vague
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-2}

\begin{quote}
In the wavenumber representation, the position--momentum uncertainty
relation becomes Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{k} ≥ 1/2, described as a lower bound on
the product of conjugate quantities in Fourier transforms. Planck's
constant appears as a dimensional conversion factor.
\end{quote}

\textbf{More rigorously}: ℏ is the quantum of action appearing in the
canonical commutation relation {[}\emph{x}, \emph{p}{]} = iℏ, and has
physical content independent of the fact that ℏ = 1 can be set in
natural units. The present paper's claim is restricted to the fact of
rewriting: the transition to the wavenumber representation rewrites the
canonical commutation relation as {[}\emph{x}, \emph{k}{]} = i, and
allows the uncertainty relation to be read as a geometric inequality of
wavenumber conjugacy. ``ℏ disappears'' means it disappears from the
surface of the expression; it does not deny the physical role of ℏ as
the quantum of action.

\subsection{Thought Experiment IV: Quantum Correlations as a Composite
Wave
Packet}\label{thought-experiment-iv-quantum-correlations-as-a-composite-wave-packet}

\subsubsection{Setting}\label{setting-3}

Consider a state localized in two spatial regions \emph{A} and \emph{B}
with correlations mediated by a shared conserved quantity (momentum,
energy, etc.). In standard descriptions, this system is treated as a
``two-particle entangled state'' {[}3, 4, 5{]}.

We attempt to describe this instead as ``a state in which a single
composite wave packet is localized in two regions in space.''

\subsubsection{String Analogy}\label{string-analogy}

When a long, taut string undergoing baseline vibration has one end
suddenly fixed, a soliton-like deformation appears at the opposite end.
This is a result of the entire string obeying conservation laws as a
single system; no information has propagated from one end to the other.

\subsubsection{Geometry of the Composite Wave
Packet}\label{geometry-of-the-composite-wave-packet}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The composite wave packet occupies one area element in phase space
\item
  In configuration space it has localized peaks in two regions \emph{A}
  and \emph{B}
\item
  A local interaction occurs on the \emph{A} side, contracting
  Δ\emph{x}\_A
\item
  Due to the Robertson inequality {[}6{]} and
  Robertson--Schrödinger-type covariance constraints, a reduction of
  spread along one projection direction induces compensating changes in
  the conjugate direction or covariance component, broadening
  Δ\emph{p}\_A
\item
  To satisfy the geometric constraints from the symplectic capacity of
  the entire composite packet, the spread of the wave packet on the
  \emph{B} side also undergoes geometric deformation
\end{itemize}

However, the ``deformation on the \emph{B} side'' referred to here is a
change in the description of the post-measurement state
\emph{conditioned on the measurement result on the A side}, and does not
mean that the unconditioned reduced density matrix on the \emph{B} side
changes under local operations on the \emph{A} side. This distinction
maintains consistency with the no-signalling theorem of standard quantum
theory.

In this description, no information transfer from \emph{A} to \emph{B}
is introduced. The observed correlation is a geometric consequence
reflecting the state of the entire system, conserved quantities and
symmetries, and the symplectic-capacity constraints.

\subsubsection{The True Nature of the Geometric
Invariant}\label{the-true-nature-of-the-geometric-invariant}

What constrains the geometry of the composite system is not individual
wavelengths or positions, but the \textbf{symplectic capacity} formed by
the product of the spreads of conjugate physical quantities (an area
element in phase space):

\[\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}, \quad \Delta A \cdot \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}\left|\langle [A,B] \rangle\right|.\]

The left side has the dimension of area; the inequality means a lower
bound on the area. This is not a simple conservation law of
Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{p}, but should be understood as a geometric constraint
by the symplectic capacity, which is invariant under canonical
transformations.

Strictly, in multi-degree-of-freedom systems, the symplectic capacity is
not defined as a simple total phase-space volume or as the product of
component uncertainty products, but as a symplectic invariant concerning
projections onto canonical 2-planes (Gromov's non-squeezing theorem
{[}15{]}). In the present paper, we use the Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{p}-type area
element as its intuitive shadow that conveys the essence.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-3}

\begin{quote}
Quantum correlations are describable as ``a state in which one composite
wave packet is localized across multiple regions in space.'' The
observed correlations are geometric consequences reflecting the state of
the entire system, conserved quantities and symmetries, and the
symplectic-capacity constraints; this description does not require
information transfer between spatially separated systems.
\end{quote}

\textbf{More rigorously}: the composite wave packet picture of the
present paper is an attempt to read a single state vector on the
tensor-product Hilbert space of standard quantum theory through the
geometric intuition of phase space. The string-soliton analogy provides
an intuitive presentation of the geometric structure of conservation
laws; it is not a complete re-derivation of the quantitative predictions
of Bell-inequality violations, for example the maximal violation 2√2 of
the CHSH inequality (the Tsirelson bound). Those quantitative
predictions follow from the algebraic structure of quantum theory
independently of any classical wave analogy. The present description's
``no information transfer required'' property is consistent with the
no-signalling theorem of standard quantum theory (partial trace of the
density operator, invariance under local operations).

\subsection{Thought Experiment V: The Algebra of
Observables}\label{thought-experiment-v-the-algebra-of-observables}

\textbf{Preface on the validity range of metaphors}: In this section, we
use the intuitive terms ``wave packet,'' ``area element,'' and
``projection.'' These are valid as semiclassical and geometric metaphors
for pure states in position-momentum systems (infinite-dimensional,
continuous spectrum). For spin systems and finite-dimensional systems,
they should be reread as ``wave packet = point on projective Hilbert
space, state direction'' and ``area element = geometry of the
non-commutative algebra of projection operators.'' Each ``More
rigorously'' section at the end of the thought experiments supplements
the strict mathematical correspondences of these expressions.

\subsubsection{Setting}\label{setting-4}

We list several pairs of physical quantities that satisfy the
uncertainty relation Δ\emph{A}Δ\emph{B} ≥
\textbar⟨{[}\emph{A},\emph{B}{]}⟩\textbar/2:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Position \emph{x} ↔ momentum \emph{p}
\item
  Spin \emph{S}\_x ↔ \emph{S}\_y ↔ \emph{S}\_z
\item
  Polarization (H/V ↔ D/A ↔ R/L)
\item
  Angular momentum \emph{L}\_x ↔ \emph{L}\_y ↔ \emph{L}\_z
\item
  Energy ↔ time (note: the energy-time uncertainty relation has a
  different mathematical status from Robertson-type uncertainty
  relations between position-momentum or spin components, because time
  is not generally defined as a self-adjoint operator in standard
  quantum mechanics)
\end{itemize}

All of these share a common structure.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-4}

\begin{quote}
Physical quantities satisfying an uncertainty relation are described as
distinct projections of the same wave packet (eigenvector). The product
of non-commuting projections is bounded below by the area element of the
wave packet (Robertson inequality).

In the geometric reading of the present paper, a physical quantity is
understood as an observable that extracts a real-valued distribution
from a state, and its value is obtained through the measurement basis or
spectral projection. A wave packet is interpreted, in the semiclassical
phase-space representation, as an area element whose spread is
constrained by Robertson--Schrödinger-type covariance constraints and by
the symplectic capacity. The projections of observables do not take
arbitrary independent values, but are correlated under the geometric
invariants of the same state.
\end{quote}

The area-element picture of the present paper is consistent with de
Gosson's \emph{quantum blobs} {[}9{]} --- the smallest phase-space units
saturating the Robertson--Schrödinger inequality, measured by symplectic
capacity, that is, an area-type invariant on canonical 2-planes. The
present paper can be regarded as a reading of Chapter 1 of Shimizu's
textbook that reconstructs this mathematically heavy
symplectic-geometric framework in the intuitive form of thought
experiments. Related geometrical formulations include the
Kähler-geometric formulation on projective Hilbert space {[}11{]} and
the algebraic approach in which the algebra of observables is taken as
primary {[}12{]}.

\textbf{More rigorously}: ``operator = projection'' risks confusion with
\emph{projection operator} as a special concept; observables are
\emph{self-adjoint operators}, and in projective measurements values are
extracted via spectral projections (for generalized measurements,
POVMs). ``Eigenvector = wave packet'' is an effective metaphor for
position-momentum systems, but for spin systems and finite-dimensional
systems it should be interpreted as ``a state direction, a point in
projective Hilbert space.'' The phase-space area-element picture of the
present paper is a semiclassical and geometric interpretation; rigorous
formulations purely on Hilbert space are given by Weyl quantization,
Wigner functions {[}13{]}, Moyal brackets {[}14{]}, and geometric
quantization.

Note that the Robertson inequality itself is a lower-bound condition:
for position and momentum in infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, there
is no general absolute upper bound on (Δ\emph{x})(Δ\emph{p}) (in a
free-particle Gaussian wave packet, Δ\emph{x} can grow with time).
However, in finite-dimensional systems, bounded observables, and
state-dependent contexts, \emph{reverse uncertainty relations} with
upper-bound-type uncertainty relations have been studied in recent
years. The geometric-invariant claim of the present paper is most
accurately read not as a simple conservation law of Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{p},
but as the invariance of the symplectic capacity under canonical
transformations (de Gosson's quantum blob {[}9{]}, Gromov's
non-squeezing theorem {[}15{]}).

\subsection{Summary}\label{summary}

Through the five thought experiments, the following structure emerged:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The identification wall} (I) --- Δ is not a resolution but a
  threshold of individuation. \emph{L} ≥ Δ is a prerequisite for
  distance measurement.
\item
  \textbf{Indistinguishability of the locus of fluctuation} (II) --- Δ
  on the measure side and Δ on the quantity side cannot be distinguished
  from observed data.
\item
  \textbf{Uncertainty relation in the wavenumber representation} (III)
  --- Δ\emph{x}Δ\emph{k} ≥ 1/2 is a mathematical fact of Fourier
  transforms. ℏ appears as a unit conversion factor.
\item
  \textbf{Quantum correlations as a composite wave packet} (IV) ---
  Described as the spatial division of a single wave packet, the
  correlations between spatially separated systems can be explained
  without assuming information transfer.
\item
  \textbf{The algebra of observables} (V) --- Physical quantities
  satisfying an uncertainty relation are distinct projections of the
  same wave packet. The area element in phase space (symplectic
  capacity) acts as a geometric invariant that imposes constraints under
  canonical transformations.
\end{enumerate}

These are consistent with the framework of complex Hilbert spaces,
observables, and measurement introduced in Chapters 1 to 3 of Shimizu's
\emph{New Edition: Foundations of Quantum Theory}, and present one
reading that provides a conceptual outlook on quantum theory. The
present paper does not modify the mathematical predictions of standard
quantum theory; it confines itself to offering a geometric
interpretation of each concept.

\subsection{Open Problems and Future
Work}\label{open-problems-and-future-work}

To develop the thought experiments of the present paper into a fully
mathematical formulation, the following routes are possible:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Formulating Thought Experiments I and II as POVM /
  coarse-grained measurement}: rigorously treating the identification
  wall and the indistinguishability of fluctuation locus within the
  framework of Kraus operators and generalized measurement, and
  clarifying the relation to decoherence theory when the apparatus
  Hamiltonian is known.
\item
  \textbf{Formulating Thought Experiment III via Fourier analysis and
  the Robertson inequality}: deriving the position-wavenumber conjugacy
  from the Plancherel theorem and clarifying ℏ's role as an
  action-dimensional coefficient.
\item
  \textbf{Formulating Thought Experiment IV via tensor-product space,
  partial trace, and the no-signalling theorem}: refining the composite
  wave packet picture as the geometry of reduced states of density
  operators, and quantitatively deriving the maximal CHSH violation 2√2
  (the Tsirelson bound) for Bell-inequality violations.
\item
  \textbf{Formulating Thought Experiment V via C*-algebras / von Neumann
  algebras / geometrical quantum mechanics}: a self-contained
  description of the algebra of observables, and explicating its
  correspondence with the Kähler manifold structure on projective
  Hilbert space {[}11{]}.
\item
  \textbf{Explicating correspondences with de Gosson's quantum blobs
  {[}9{]}, Wigner functions {[}13{]}, and Moyal brackets {[}14{]}}:
  connecting to phase-space quantum mechanics via symplectic capacity,
  the Wigner quasi-probability distribution, and the Moyal star product.
\end{enumerate}

These are research programs that go beyond the observational framework
of the present paper and should be treated as independent successor
works.

\textbf{Priority outlook}: Of the above, the most natural successor
candidates are (1) the formulation of Thought Experiments I and II as
POVM / coarse-grained measurement, and (5) the explication of
correspondences with de Gosson's quantum blobs. The former directly
connects to standard quantum measurement theory and rigorously
formalizes the starting point of the present paper (the identification
wall and the indistinguishability of the locus of fluctuation); the
latter positions the core picture of the present paper (the phase-space
area element) within the existing framework of symplectic geometry.
Items (2), (3), and (4) can then be expanded successively on this
foundation.
