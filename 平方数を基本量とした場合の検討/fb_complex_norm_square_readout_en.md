[The conjugate complex number is really just a sum of squares]

The conjugate complex number is familiar in theoretical physics. Multiplying a complex number by its conjugate shows up everywhere, from quantum mechanics to signal processing.

In fact, that operation is very simple inside:

　(x + iy)(x − iy) = x² + y²

The imaginary unit i cancels out cleanly, and what remains is just Pythagoras (a sum of squares). The direction (phase) drops away, and only the squared magnitude is left —— that is all.

Extend this to many dimensions, keeping every axis on an equal footing, and bundling conjugate complex numbers ends up as "the sum of the squares of all coordinates = a single squared radius." No axis is treated specially.

This is not a new law of physics — just a short observational note that rearranges already-known mathematics into a clearer view. ...Though, at the very end, it also touches a small algebraic twist: taking a coordinate to be imaginary flips the sign of its square, because i² = −1. Readers who know some physics may find themselves raising an eyebrow there.
