Observation as Simultaneous Emission and Absorption: Deriving the Born Rule, Bell Correlations and the Tsirelson Bound from a Viewing-Angle Readout Rule

観測は放射と吸収の同時成立である ― 視野角と大きさの読出し規則からボルン則・ベル相関・ツィレルソン限界を導く ―

Noriaki Kihara (WF System Co., Ltd.) · ORCID 0009-0004-6753-4020 · 2026-11-01 · v1.4 · Series: Generative Structure of Dimensions

Abstract

What quantum mechanics calls "observation" has lumped together three interactions of different character: one that switches the discrete state of the partner, one that leaves the partner's state unswitched and shifts only the internal phases, and one that severs the mutual phase relation. This paper places the substance of observation in the second, the readout of the interference of a phase difference, and adopts only three premises. (P1) The distance between two parties is set by the ratio of a shared viewing angle to the partner's size; only ratios are readable and the absolute scale is not. (P2) An interaction is a single relation in which emission and absorption hold simultaneously; the speed of light appears on the readout side as a conversion factor between local clocks. (P3) The phase of a relation carries a harmonic order n that depends on the kind of wave. From these, the fact that a probability is the product of an amplitude and its conjugate (the form of the Born rule), the two-party correlation E = ∓cos nθ, the upper bound 2√2 of the CHSH quantity (the Tsirelson bound), and no-signaling follow in a few lines. The normalization that turns this into a probability is the division that discards the size ratio, i.e. the choice of the η = 0 section. As a consequence, the bound is 2√2 independently of the kind of wave, and the detector angle giving the maximal violation shrinks in proportion to 1/n (45° for spin-½, 22.5° for photons, 11.25° for gravitational waves). For a localized relation that is a sum of harmonics the correlation is the autocorrelation of the pulse shape; the bound holds for any weights, equality is attained only for a single harmonic, and a rectangular pulse reduces to a local hidden-variable model with S ≤ 2.
