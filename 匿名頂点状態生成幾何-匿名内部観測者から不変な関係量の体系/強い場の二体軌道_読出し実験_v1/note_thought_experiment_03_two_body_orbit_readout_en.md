# I Tried to Read Out Two Bodies, and the Observer Appeared as a Third State - Reading the Mass Ratio, the Distance and the Time from an Orbit and a Gravitational Wave, the Third Thought Experiment

## This continues from the last article

In the last article, keeping two values whose meaning had not yet been decided, I read out a Coulomb-type inverse-square force from a linear rule.

Attraction, repulsion and neutrality came out of one and the same mechanism, without putting in separate rules for them.

At the same time, what two values cannot write also became clear.

The mass ratio cannot be read.  
The charge ratio cannot be read.  
The two bodies cannot even be placed on the same map and compared.

I ended the last article with this line.

"Next is three bodies."

This article is the continuation.

But I did not set out to solve the three-body problem from the start.

I tried to read out the state of two bodies without being told the answer from outside.

And then an observer became necessary.

And if that observer itself is counted as a state, the system already had three states.

---

## The question this time is: can the state of two bodies be read back from their orbit?

Suppose two celestial bodies A and B are in motion.

On the generating side, the mass ratio, the shape of the orbit, the time scale and the distance are fixed, and the observational data are made.

But the answer is not handed to the readout side.

What the readout side knows is the system of units

G = c = M = 1

and a known model of the gravity of two bodies.

And only two kinds of observation are handed over.

The first is the positions of the two bodies A and B on the celestial sphere, as seen by a distant observer C.

The second is the two polarizations of the gravitational wave that the binary emits.

From these observations alone,

Which one is the heavier?  
What is the mass ratio?  
What shape is the orbit?  
How does the distance between the two bodies change with time?  
How differently do the two clocks run?

I examined whether it is possible to read back that far.

---

## This does not mean knowing nothing

This is the point I distinguished most carefully this time.

What the readout side does not know is the values of a and b used on the generating side, and the answers such as the mass ratio.

It is not that the laws of physics are unknown too.

The readout uses known gravitational theory: the two-body motion at 2PN, the orbital decay by gravitational radiation at 2.5PN, the center-of-mass relation, the waveform of the gravitational wave, and the rate at which proper time runs.

So what I examined this time is the problem of whether the state can be computed backwards from the observations, under the condition

"the law is known, but what state the system is actually in is not known".

The generated answer files are not looked at until the readout is finished.

They are checked only at the very end.

---

## Seeing the two bodies separately, the mass ratio could be read

The first observation is simple.

From the observer C, the positions of A and B are followed on the same celestial coordinates.

The two bodies move around their center of mass.

The heavy body moves near the center of mass, in a small path.  
The light body moves far from it, in a large path.

If the two can be compared on the same coordinates with the same clock, the mass ratio can be read from the asymmetry of those motions.

This is what could not be read in the previous thought experiment.

Last time there was only "the relative motion of two bodies".

Looking at the relative motion alone, the two masses gather into a single combination, and how much heavier which one is cannot be separated.

This time it is different.

The observer C provided a reference on which A and B can be compared on the same coordinates.

With that additional relational information, the mass ratio became readable.

---

## The mass ratio was also written into the strong-field orbit itself

Putting in the corrections of general relativity, information about the mass ratio also appears in the relative orbit itself.

The direction of the periastron advances little by little.

By emitting gravitational waves, the period of the orbit becomes shorter little by little.

How fast it advances and how fast it shrinks depend not only on the size of the orbit but also on the combination of the two masses.

So I read the time series of

how far the periastron advances,  
how much the period shortens,  
how the periastron and apastron distances change,

and fitted it to the known two-body model.

The same holds for the gravitational wave.

From the strength of the wave, its phase and the change of its period, the shape of the orbit and the combination of the masses are computed backwards.

---

## I tried three different binaries

In the numerical experiment I prepared three cases with different mass ratios.

Mass ratio 1.  
Mass ratio 2.  
Mass ratio 4.

The readout side is not told these values.

In that state, I tried

the method that reads from the positions of the two bodies on the celestial sphere, and  
the method that reads from the gravitational wave alone,

separately.

As a result, the mass ratio, the shape of the orbit, the time scale, the distance, the relative distance, and the difference of the two proper times could be reconstructed.

For most of the quantities, the relative error against the answer was roughly 10^-4 to 10^-3.

## I also left one conspicuously bad result

Not everything was read with the same accuracy.

In the case of equal masses, the step that converts the quantity read from the gravitational wave back into the mass ratio q became unstable, and a discrepancy of about 6 per cent remained.

This is not so much a complete failure of the computation as the fact that, in the equal-mass limit, a small error in the intermediate quantity is greatly amplified when it is converted into q.

I left this result as it is.

As in the two previous papers, I have not selected only the results that worked.

What can be read, and where the readout becomes weak, are both results of this experiment.

---

## And then the observer C became the problem

This is the part I found most interesting this time.

At first I had placed C as a mere observer.

It is placed far enough from A and B, at a position that looks at the orbital plane of the two bodies almost face on.

Seen from C, A and B fall within a very small angle.

In the setting of this paper, that viewing angle is at most about 2×10^-4 rad.

On the other hand, seen from A and B, C lies in a direction almost at right angles to the direction joining the two bodies.

[Image position: the hierarchy of the three relations (schematic)  
observer_geometry_theta_acb_schematic_en.png]

That is,

from C, the small difference between A and B can be read.

But C is very far from the internal relative motion of A and B.

By this geometry, the relation of the two bodies can be read out while the influence on the two bodies is kept small.

In this paper I treat C as a "test observer", and I neglected the back-reaction that C itself exerts on the motion of A and B.

---

## But isn't C a state too?

Here I go back to the original design document.

A state was not something to which the names "electron", "planet" or "measuring device" were attached from the start.

It is something distinguishable that has degrees of freedom.

Seen that way, the observer C too

has a position.  
has an orientation.  
has a clock.

It is one state.

Then the system of this paper in fact has three states,

A  
B  
C

And the relations too become three,

A and B  
A and C  
B and C

I was only trying to read out the state of two bodies, and a third state came in of its own accord.

---

## Here I do not go on to the three-body problem

But in this paper I stopped here.

I have not put the three states including C all inside the same dynamics.

C is placed as a test observer, far enough away and with small enough influence.

What happens when A, B and C influence one another, and the three relations

A-B  
A-C  
B-C

evolve in time at the same time?

That is not the problem of this paper.

It is the next thought experiment.

At the end of the last article I wrote "next is three bodies".

This time I did not go straight to solving the equations of motion of three bodies.

Simply by trying to observe two bodies properly, a third state - the observer - became necessary first.

That is where the entrance to the three-body problem was.

---

## What was found

Joining the results of this time into one line, it comes out like this.

There were quantities that cannot be read from the relative motion of two bodies alone.

Adding there an observer C with common coordinates and a common clock, the state of the two bodies including the mass ratio could be read out.

And if C itself is counted as a state, the system was already a three-state system.

That is, the problem I had been considering as

"how to move two bodies"

changed into the problem of

"how to close the relations among three states".

Next I go on to that.
