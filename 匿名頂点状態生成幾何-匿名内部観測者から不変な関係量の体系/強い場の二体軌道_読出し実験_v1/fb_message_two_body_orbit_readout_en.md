Reading out the mass ratio, the relative distance and the relative time just by observing the orbit of two celestial bodies

Details in the comments

All the readout side holds is the system of units G = c = M = 1 and a known model of two-body gravity
What the two values a, b are is zero knowledge, and the truth was not looked at until the final cross-check
Only two kinds of observation: the positions of the two bodies on the celestial sphere seen by a distant observer C, and the two polarizations of the gravitational wave
The distance, the time scale, the origin of time, the orientation of the celestial sphere and which body is heavier were all hidden
All three cases, with mass ratios 1, 2 and 4, could be read out, most relative errors being 10^-4 to 10^-3
Which of the two bodies is heavier could also be decided, by comparing them on the same coordinates with the same clock
The mass ratio, which the previous paper had fixed as unwritable with two values, became readable through the additional relational information given by the observer C, and because in a strong field the mass ratio enters the relative motion itself
There is a bad result too: with equal masses the mass ratio from the gravitational wave alone is off by about 6 per cent, and the bias in the eccentricity read from the wave has not gone away
The readout uses the same equations of motion as the generation, taken as known; robustness against model error has not been tested
Simply trying to read out two bodies made a third state, the observer, necessary: that is where the entrance to the three-body problem was
