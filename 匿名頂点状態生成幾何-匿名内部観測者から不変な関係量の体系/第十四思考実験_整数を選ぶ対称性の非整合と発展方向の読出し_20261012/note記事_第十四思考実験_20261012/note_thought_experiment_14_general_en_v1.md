# Why Don't the Three 137s Agree Exactly? - Search for Pairs That Are Close but Never Lock: the Fourteenth Thought Experiment

Physics loves symmetry.

General relativity and the Standard Model are full of symmetries.

Laws that do not change when spacetime is rotated or shifted.

Laws that do not change when the phase of the electron is rotated.

Laws that do not change when the colors of quarks are exchanged.

And Noether's theorem tells us:

if there is a symmetry, there is a corresponding conserved quantity.

Energy, momentum and electric charge all come out of symmetries in this way.

It is a beautiful story.

There is just one thing nobody tells us.

**Why is that symmetry there?**

Why do we need so many symmetries of such different character, all at the same time?

There is no answer to this.

Symmetries are placed almost as axioms.

"In a world with these symmetries, the calculation gives this."

Please do not ask beyond that.

## I got stuck on the hydrogen atom

I ran into this question again while I was doing numerical experiments on the hydrogen atom.

Why does the electron not fall into the nucleus?

The textbook explanation goes like this.

If the electron comes closer to the nucleus, its position becomes sharper.

Then, by the uncertainty principle, its momentum spreads out and its kinetic energy grows.

Coming too close costs more than it gains, so it settles at a suitable distance.

As a calculation, this explanation is correct.

But look closely: Planck's constant ℏ and the uncertainty principle are put in as inputs from the start.

If you put the ingredients of the answer into the input, the answer comes out.

That is less an explanation than a confirmation.

What I wanted to ask lay one step earlier.

**Why is quantum structure itself necessary?**

## Three 137s in three papers

Let me tell you about my own past research here.

The fine-structure constant α is the number that expresses how strongly the electron and light are coupled.

Its inverse is, by observation,

　α⁻¹ = 137.035999177…

It is one of the most famous numbers in physics.

Over the past half year, in separate papers, I ran into the neighborhood of this number three times.

- Counting the unit cells of a four-dimensional lattice that fit completely inside a ball of radius 3 gives exactly 137 (BH thermodynamics, Paper 3)
- Solving an equation made from that 137 and the volume of the four-dimensional ball gives 137.036010988… (BH Paper 7)
- In an experiment where two waves collide again and again, the value made from the reflectance at which the state returns after exactly 124 collisions is 137.036042915… (the paper of July 18)

All three come from completely different lines of thought.

And yet all of them sit near 137.03….

It would be impressive to say "I have finally derived α", but unfortunately I cannot.

Compared with the observed value, they are off at the fifth or sixth decimal place.

That is far more than measurement error can explain.

So I cannot say "they agree".

But I found myself more interested in the way they are off.

**Values close to each other come out of theories that have nothing to do with each other. But they never agree exactly.**

Is this really a coincidence?

## Perhaps not agreeing exactly is what matters

I tried changing the way I think about it.

Suppose several symmetries each select a number close to the others.

If those numbers agreed exactly, the two symmetries would mesh completely, and nothing would happen.

A quiet world in which everything is fixed.

If, on the other hand, the numbers were completely unrelated, they would not affect each other either.

So what happens when they are close but do not agree exactly?

The two pull each other in.

But they never mesh completely.

Then the system stays almost meshed for a long time, and every now and then it suddenly slips by one step.

It settles again for a while, and slips again.

This state of "staying long, and changing suddenly from time to time" is called metastable in physics.

In fact, something very much like this is well known in mathematics.

When you shake a pendulum periodically, its swing can be pulled into a whole-number ratio with the period of the shaking.

Near the edge of the range where it is pulled in, long stretches of almost perfect synchronization alternate with occasional slips of one full turn.

Each slip happens as a single "one", with no fractions.

So I thought:

**perhaps this relation, pulling in but never locking completely, is what looks to us like the ticks of time and space, like discreteness, like uncertainty.**

And perhaps the observed constants are what we get when we read the history of that wobbling, accumulated from a particular direction.

Perhaps whatever goes back and forth among the three 137s only looks like the single value printed in textbooks when read along the direction of evolution.

Of course, this is a hypothesis.

## Impose two conditions at once, and integers are selected

"Symmetries select integers" may sound suspicious.

But mathematics has solid examples. And in every one of them, integers appear only when two conditions are imposed at the same time.

The first is crystals.

The rotations that bring a crystal back onto itself are only 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold.

With rotation alone, any rotation is possible.

With regular arrangement alone, nothing is forbidden.

But demand both at once, and only the integers 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 remain.

What is interesting is that a material with 5-fold symmetry, which should be incompatible, was actually found in 1984.

The quasicrystal.

Demanding incompatible symmetries at the same time did not produce a mess. Instead, a different kind of order appeared, like a shadow of a crystal in a higher dimension.

The second is multiplication of numbers.

Number systems in which multiplication and length (the norm) are both neatly compatible exist only in 1, 2, 4 and 8 dimensions.

The real numbers, complex numbers, quaternions and octonions.

In 16 dimensions, they are no longer compatible.

The third is the symmetry called E₈.

It is one of the most famous symmetries "with a magic number" in physics.

Impose two conditions on a lattice, and E₈ is the one and only lattice selected in 8 dimensions.

And E₈ does not appear in the everyday laws of physics.

Yet in 2010, when a certain magnet was placed under special conditions, the ratio of the masses of the vibrations inside it was observed to approach the golden ratio 1.618…, exactly as E₈ predicts.

It appears only when two conditions are combined: placing the magnet at its critical point, and nudging it slightly away with a weak magnetic field.

Symmetry is not placed at the root as a single thing; it is picked out from combinations of conditions.

That is what this example looks like.

By the way, the three 137s also share numbers with this E₈.

The size of E₈ is 248, which splits as 248 = 8 + 112 + 128.

The 137 of the four-dimensional cells splits as 137 = 1 + 8 + 128.

8 and 128 are shared.

But the shapes inside the 128 are different.

On the side of the reflectance that returns after 124 collisions, 124 = 4 × 31, and 31 is just 1 more than 30, a characteristic number of E₈.

Again, close, but not an exact match.

At this point, calling it a coincidence almost takes more courage.

## So, what to search for

What I most want to say with this thought experiment is this.

**Let us search for pairs that look theoretically unrelated, yet sit close to each other, and pull each other in without ever locking completely.**

There is no need to explain why they are close.

Being close but not identical is itself the clue.

Conversely, if a reason for an exact match is found, this hypothesis loses.

If the two lock completely, neither metastability nor sudden change can arise.

Physicists usually look for "why things agree".

I decided to bet on "why they do not agree" instead.

## What to check next

- Show concretely where the two symmetries collide on the same state
- Compute whether long stays and occasional slips really occur without a hidden clock
- Search widely, beyond 137, for pairs that are close but do not agree
- If an exactly matching correspondence is found, remove that pair from the candidates

## Just one more thing

At the end of the paper, I also wrote about the symmetry I place at the very top of my own theoretical system.

It is a one-line equation: a sum of squares equals zero.

This equation is the very shape of the cone along which light travels.

And here is where it is easily misunderstood: this "zero" does not mean that a conserved quantity is zero.

A conserved quantity of any size can be rewritten in this form by adding one more axis.

So the equation does not fix the value of a conserved quantity; it only says that a conserved quantity can exist.

Moreover, this form is not placed as an assumption. It comes out automatically when you square numbers in which multiplication and length are compatible.

If you are interested, please see Chapter 11 of the paper.
