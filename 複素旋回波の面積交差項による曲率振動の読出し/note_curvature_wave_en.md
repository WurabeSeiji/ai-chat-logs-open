# What is a complex wave doing on a geodesic in curved spacetime?

## Trigger

I was thinking about complex-valued waves propagating along geodesics in curved spacetime.

Then something occurred to me.

The direction of the radius of curvature R—that direction is unobservable directly from the geodesic.

Just as we use imaginary numbers i to represent time (an unobservable direction), shouldn't we use imaginary numbers for the direction of radius of curvature R as well?

## Reformulating the spatial equation

A four-dimensional spacetime with radius of curvature R is expressed as:

$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - t^2 = R^2$

But when curvature is present, if we bring the curvature radius term R to the left-hand side and treat it as imaginary:

$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + (it)^2 + (iR)^2 = 0$

Ah, this takes the form of a sum of squares equal to zero.

The axiom from my series:

$\sum x_n^2 = 0$

This means that from the perspective of the geodesic, the curvature radius R direction appears as an imaginary direction.

## Squaring a complex wave

This is where it gets important.

When we square a wave represented by a complex number $a + ib$:

$(a + ib)^2 = a^2 - b^2 + 2iab$

This is a straightforward algebraic expansion.

But wait a moment.

The cross term $2iab$ carries a factor of i.

## Values in the imaginary direction

The real part $a^2 - b^2$ can be understood as the squared amplitude in the real domain (geodesic direction).

Then what about $2iab$?

This is a value in the imaginary direction perpendicular to the geodesic—in other words, in the direction of the radius of curvature R.

If something is being transmitted in the direction of radius of curvature R, that something might be this cross term.

## Insight

So what exactly is the value $2iab$ being transmitted in the direction of radius of curvature R?

I started thinking.

Could this be a gravitational wave?

## Investigation

I decided to look into it.

Characteristics of gravitational waves:

• Frequency emerges from the calculation

• Amplitude emerges at the same order as observation

• Double-angle polarization structure is present

What emerges from the cross term of the complex wave:

• Area oscillations in the imaginary direction

• Double-angle structure appears automatically

• Frequency is determined once the curvature scale is specified

Surprisingly similar.

## The power of simplicity

No general relativity. No standard model.

Just:

• Space has radius of curvature R

• A complex wave propagates there

• We look at the cross term of its square

• We capture the imaginary direction

That's it.

A method this simple—almost absurdly simple—yet it can express gravitational wave-like behavior.

## Why does this happen?

Geodesics represent the observable direction, and curvature represents the unobservable direction.

Between these two, the mathematical structure of complex numbers builds a bridge.

The cross term that automatically emerges from squaring a complex number is precisely suited to express waves transmitted from unobservable directions.

Perhaps formal algebraic play has touched upon the essence of the phenomenon itself.
