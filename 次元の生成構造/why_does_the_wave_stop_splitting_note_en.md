# Why does the wave stop splitting? — At the moment it stops, new directions were being born

Noriaki Kihara  
2026-07-25

In the previous article, "Why do the directions stop at three, and why do waves increase on their own?", I reported the following result.

In a system that places no background space from the start and begins only from the relations among waves, a single wave splits on its own — amplifying exponentially and abruptly, from a seed too small to measure, in a way reminiscent of the inflation of the early universe.

But this amplification does not continue forever.

At a certain time it stops increasing, and thereafter moves into a metastable state that keeps fluctuating within a finite range.

Here one big puzzle remained.

**Why does the splitting of the wave stop on its own?**

If the single wave itself is unstable, the splitting might well continue indefinitely.

And yet the amplification stops somewhere, and the system moves to a new state. At that moment, what is happening inside?

In this paper I investigated this "stopping process" directly.

The result exceeded my expectations.

At the same moment splitting stops, several orthogonal directions that were almost empty until then stood up with finite amplitude.

In one line:

**The splitting of a single wave stops together with the birth of new orthogonal directions.**

Let me report in order.

## Amplification arises from the system looking at itself

First, I checked where the amplification comes from.

In this system, the way the wave rotates is rebuilt at each step from the current state. In the paper this rotation rule is called the "generator."

That is, the state reads its own phases, decides the next rotation from those phases, and that rotation changes the state again.

The system moves while referring to itself.

So I ran an experiment that stops this self-reference.

I fixed the rotation rule at the initial state and did not rebuild it afterward.

Then the tiny dormant seed did not grow at all.

Even after 2000 steps it stayed at about 10 to the minus 27 in energy ratio, barely changing.

But when I stopped fixing it and rebuilt the rotation from the state at each step, the same seed amplified by about 30 orders of magnitude and reached a finite size.

So the source of amplification is not the rotation prepared in advance.

The state reads its own phases and rebuilds the rotation, and that rotation changes the state again.

This self-referential loop was amplifying the dormant component.

**The system looking at itself increases the waves.**

## The reason it stops was not fuel exhaustion

The simplest explanation is that it stopped because it used up the original wave.

Each time a new wave grows, the original wave decreases, and finally the material for splitting runs out.

If so, at the time amplification stops, almost none of the original wave should remain.

But in large systems it was completely different.

Measuring how much remained in the initial dominant direction after it stopped gave the following.

- In the 5-body system, about 20% remained in the original dominant direction.
- In the 40-body system, about 80% remained.
- In the 300-body system, about 91% remained.

For 5 bodies, a fair amount of the original wave is used.

But for 40 or 300 bodies, the splitting quantity stops at a finite value while most of the original dominant direction remains.

There is still plenty of fuel.

And yet it stops.

Therefore the arrest of splitting was not a simple fuel exhaustion.

Then where did the amplified component go?

## At the moment it stops, new directions stand up

The motion of this system can be read as several mutually orthogonal rotation planes plus a non-rotating region.

In three dimensions, a single rotation axis corresponds to a rotation plane.

But in general higher dimensions, "in which two-dimensional plane it is rotating" is more fundamental than an axis.

At the start of the experiment, the state is almost localized in a single dominant rotation plane.

This corresponds to the single wave.

However, it is not exactly zero.

Outside the dominant plane there is a seed too small to measure.

Once self-reference begins, this out-of-plane component amplifies exponentially.

And at the time the splitting quantity moves into the metastable regime, the component that flowed out of the dominant plane settled with finite size into another rotation subspace and into the non-rotating region.

(Insert Figure 2 here)

![At the same time it stops, amplitude flows into new directions](第6論文_figures_v1/planeflow_N00005_exact.png)

Figure 2 is the 5-body system. Blue is the initial dominant plane, orange is the other rotation subspaces, green is the non-rotating region. At the same time the splitting quantity rises, outflow from the dominant plane to the other directions begins, and thereafter it moves to a finite distribution.

There is a clear relation here.

**The splitting quantity is exactly the fraction of the conserved quantity that flowed out of the initial dominant plane.**

This agreement was confirmed to a number of digits close to the limit of numerical precision.

So the waves increased by splitting did not pile up into the same single direction.

They flowed out of the dominant plane and came to occupy, with finite amplitude, an orthogonal direction that had been almost unoccupied.

The metastable state is not simply a state where splitting has weakened.

**It is the state after the system has acquired new directions.**

## The same thing happened in large systems

This phenomenon was not a special feature of small systems only.

For 300 bodies, the number of relational waves is 44,850.

Also, the rotation subspaces other than the dominant plane amount to 598 dimensions. Counted as two-dimensional rotation planes, that is 299 planes.

Even in that huge system the basic structure was the same.

From the dominant plane, the conserved quantity flows into the other rotation subspaces and the non-rotating region, and moves to a finite distribution.

## The boundary where new directions become available was between 4 and 5 bodies

In the previous paper I found that the boundary where sustained splitting occurs lies between 4 and 5 bodies.

For 3 and 4 bodies, splitting does not persist.

For 5 bodies and above, within the range examined, the new wave settled to a finite size.

This time, examining the structure of rotation planes, there was another boundary at the same place.

For 3 and 4 bodies, there is no second nonzero rotation plane other than the dominant plane.

At 5 bodies, a second nonzero rotation plane appears for the first time.

That is,

- the boundary where the second rotation plane appears
- the boundary where sustained splitting becomes possible

coincided.

This coincidence suggests that the second rotation plane may be the "escape destination" necessary for sustained splitting.

Without a place to escape into, a tiny component, even if amplified, may not be able to settle as a finite new state.

However, I have not proven the sufficient condition that splitting always occurs whenever a second rotation plane exists.

What can be said for certain at present is that the two boundaries coincided.

## In the metastable state, the amplitude is thinly distributed across the whole system

After settling into the metastable state, I also examined the amplitude of each relational wave.

Then the representative amplitude per relational wave approached

**one divided by the square root of the number of relational waves.**

The number of relational waves, with N bodies, is N times N minus one divided by two.

From 3 to 300 bodies, 18 sizes, 38 runs in total, I confirmed this relation.

The larger the system, the more rapidly the number of relational waves increases.

Since the total conserved quantity is constant, when it is distributed over many relations, the amplitude per relation becomes small.

So it is not that no change occurs in large systems.

**Because the change is thinly distributed across many relations, it becomes hard to see from any single component.**

## Comparing the exact method and the approximate method

To examine the inflow into rotation planes, I used two computational methods.

One is to build the generator matrix directly and diagonalize it.

For 5 and 40 bodies, I obtained the rotation subspaces and the non-rotating region directly by this method.

The other is a low-rank approximation for huge systems such as 300 bodies.

For 300 bodies there are 44,850 relational waves, so diagonalizing the full matrix directly is not practical in the present computing environment.

So, using the low-rank structure of the generator, I reconstructed the rotation subspaces from a small matrix.

This approximate method has a threshold for removing numerical noise.

I did not hide that value; I stated it in the program arguments, the output data, and the figure captions.

Furthermore, at 40 bodies I compared the exact and approximate methods under the same conditions.

As a result, for the projection quantities used here, the two methods agreed to about 10 to the minus 15, the level of machine precision.

Therefore, at least within the range verified at 40 bodies, I confirmed that the low-rank approximation reproduces the exact result.

Building on that verification, I applied it to 300 bodies.

## What are the newly arising directions?

Here I draw a clear line.

What I confirmed this time is:

- that another orthogonal rotation subspace exists outside the dominant plane,
- that at the same time as splitting, the conserved quantity flows into it,
- that the conserved quantity also flows into the non-rotating region,
- and that the state including these metastabilizes.

This existence and inflow are computational results inside the model; they are not hypotheses.

On the other hand, what those directions correspond to in real physics is not yet known.

Is it time?

Is it charge?

Is it another internal degree of freedom?

Can the non-rotating region be read as a physical direction carrying a conserved quantity?

These are future tasks.

In this paper I do not attach physical names in advance.

I show only that nameless new directions came to be finitely occupied together with the arrest of splitting.

## Is the metastable state the final state?

From this result, a further new question arises.

The system flowed out of the first dominant plane, and a new rotational direction corresponding to a second plane became finitely occupied.

Then, will that newly standing direction also eventually become unstable in the same way?

If so, then

from the first plane to the second,

from the second to the third,

from the third to the fourth,

new orthogonal directions might stand up in stages.

The metastable state found this time may not be the final state, but an intermediate state until the next direction stands up.

In the next paper I will investigate this possibility.

However, waiting until the third plane itself grows to a finite amplitude may require very long computation time.

So I will measure whether the metastable state, with up to the second plane standing, amplifies the tiny fluctuations outside it.

If the outer fluctuations grow, directions of the third plane and beyond may stand up.

If they shrink, the creation of directions stops there.

This test alone can distinguish

- whether it stops exactly at two planes,
- whether there is a critical size at which the third plane stands,
- whether it stands mathematically but is too slow to observe,
- or whether up to the third stands but the fourth and beyond become extremely difficult.

## The connection to three generations is still a candidate prediction

Real elementary particles have three generations.

If this model were to give a result such as

- up to the third plane stands,
- the fourth and beyond stabilize,
- or the standing-up time becomes extremely long,

then it might connect to the three-generation structure of particles.

However, this is not yet a confirmed result.

What I confirmed this time is only up to the point that finite inflow occurred from a single dominant plane into its outer orthogonal directions.

I will experiment on the third plane and beyond from here.

What matters is that this model is beginning to be more than something that merely attaches an explanation to anything after the fact.

What should be measured next.

Which result stops at two planes, and which result lets the third stand.

What result would make the fourth and beyond difficult.

It has advanced to a stage where the branches can be shown in advance.

## What can and cannot be claimed as discovered

What can be claimed as discovered this time is as follows.

- The amplification of a single wave arises from the state referring to itself and rebuilding its rotation.
- Splitting does not stop because the original wave was used up.
- The splitting quantity equals the fraction of the conserved quantity that flowed out of the dominant plane.
- The outflow destinations are another rotation subspace and the non-rotating region.
- The metastabilization of splitting and the finite occupation of new orthogonal directions occur in the same process.
- In large systems, metastabilization occurs while most of the original dominant direction remains.
- In the metastable state, the conserved quantity is thinly distributed over many relations.

On the other hand, there are things that cannot yet be claimed as discovered.

- That the new directions correspond to real time or charge.
- That directions of the third plane and beyond really stand up.
- That three-generation particles correspond to three rotation planes.
- That the fourth plane and beyond are strictly forbidden.

These are future research tasks.

## In one line

Previously I showed that

**a single wave is not stable and increases on its own.**

This time, investigating the continuation, I showed that

**the splitting of the wave stops by finitely occupying new directions.**

At least in this model, directions are not merely placed as a background from the start.

They stand up as internal directions of motion together with the splitting of the wave.

And what I will investigate next is

**whether a newly stood-up direction also gives rise to the next direction.**
