# Why Does a Wave That Should Not Move Appear to Move? — The Observer's Resolution and the Extra Dimension, Out of a Numerical Experiment That Got Stuck

I have been pursuing research that does not assume a background spacetime. I am looking for the mechanism by which spacetime arises from nothing but the relations between complex numbers, and by which particles, interactions and observation arise.

Recently I noticed that with quaternions, the three dimensions of space and time, which had troubled me for a long time, might be read out from the relations between states alone. So I built waves localised like particles by superposing quaternion harmonics, and ran numerical experiments on the readout of spacetime and on interactions.

Then I ran into a serious dead end.

Standard theory writes a wave as A e^{i(θ+ωt)}. The term ωt says that the wave travels, and it tacitly assumes that there is a time t. My theory has no background time t. So a wave is written as a standing wave without time, A e^{iθ}. The same holds for the quaternion A e^{nθ}, in which the imaginary unit i is replaced by a unit vector n in three dimensions.

Here I had been using, without much thought, the form A e^{i(θ+θ₀)}. I superposed many harmonics of a standing wave to localise it, called the position of its peak the centre phase θ₀, and moved θ₀ continuously.

On calm reflection, the assumption that θ₀ changes continuously has no basis at all. It was a hidden assumption. I decided to examine it in depth.

The present paper was born from that examination. So it proves nothing. Why can the harmonics of a standing wave, which should not move, be read out as something that moves continuously, like a particle or like space? It is a hypothesis paper that thinks about that question.

The paper is published on Zenodo (Concept DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24097611). This article follows the order in which the thinking went.

## The numerical experiment that stopped

Please look at the screen of the experiment first.

[Figure 1 here: note_fig1_experiment_two_panels.png]

Figure 1. The two panels of the numerical experiment. Left: the power of the wave on a closed circle, drawn outward from the circle. Right: the points obtained by accumulating the advance of each pulse's centre phase along the direction of that pulse's axis, and their trajectories.

In the left panel, four pulses sit on one closed circle. In the right panel, the same four look like four particles moving in a three-dimensional space.

But the three axes on the right are not coordinates of space. They are the three components of the imaginary part of a quaternion. In other words, it is a space that lays out the internal phase of the wave. That space looks like a three-dimensional space. Up to here, things were going well.

The dead end came when I made the pulses collide. However many times I rebuilt the way the interaction was put in, it never took a form I could accept. While looking for the cause, my doubt turned to something earlier: the very act of moving a pulse.

## A wave made of harmonics is a rigid crystal

On a closed string, only harmonics of integer order can stand. Order 1 has one crest on the circle, order 2 has two, order 10 has ten.

[Figure 2 here: note_fig2_first_reading_harmonics_en.png]

Figure 2. The harmonics of orders 1 to 10 on one circle (thin lines) and their sum (thick line). The sum is a peak gathered at one place.

Superposing harmonics gathers the peak at one place, as in Figure 2. This is a wave localised like a particle.

What I noticed is this. The harmonic of order 10 divides the circle into 10 parts from the outset. Order 9 divides it into 9, order 8 into 8. The finest harmonic fixes the graduation of the ruler of this world. A pulse made of harmonics up to order 10 fills space with this graduation without a gap, and has no room to shift its peak just a little.

The paper calls this a rigid crystal.

## And yet pulses appear to move continuously

If this reading were right, the universe would be a rigid crystal lattice and nothing would happen.

Yet pulses are observed to move continuously. In the experiment too, any value could be put into the centre phase θ₀ in the formula, and the pulses moved smoothly.

The formula is not wrong. The way of reading the formula is wrong somewhere.

## Reading in reverse: the base wave was the observer's resolution

The mistake was to read the longest wave, the base wave, as the wave of order 1.

What looks like the base wave is not the lowest-order wave. It is the observer's resolution. The orders of the wave in fact go on without end. A pulse shape is seen because an infinitely sharp pulse is smoothed at the resolution that can be observed.

Compare a photograph: a pattern finer than a pixel is not recorded. The finest pattern in the picture is not a property of the subject but the size of the camera's pixel.

[Figure 3 here: note_fig3_ladder_two_readings_en.png]

Figure 3. Two readings of the ladder of orders. Top: the first reading, in which the base wave is the lowest order and the harmonics stand above it. Bottom: the reverse reading, in which the wave that looks like the base wave is the observer's resolution, and waves continue on the coarser side as well.

The upper row of Figure 3 is the first reading. There is nothing on the side coarser than the base wave, and what can be read is the harmonics standing above the base wave.

The lower row is the reverse reading. The same ladder is read in the opposite direction. The side finer than the resolution is smoothed as a rigid crystal and hidden inside. What can be read is the side coarser than the resolution.

In fact, the formula used in the experiment had a form that agrees with this reading from the start. The coefficients of the pulse in the experiment coincide exactly, as a formula, with an infinitely sharp pulse in which all harmonics have the same strength, smoothed by a window of a certain width. The width of the pulse and the maximum order were not properties of the wave but quantities on the observer's side.

## Smooth a rigid crystal and it looks like a base wave

Let me draw the reverse reading. Take a large circle and superpose on it the harmonics of orders 10, 20, …, 100.

[Figure 4 here: note_fig4_rigid_crystal_lowpass_en.png]

Figure 4. The sum of orders 10, 20, …, 100 of the large circle (grey), and what remains when orders up to 10 are kept (red).

The grey line is the rigid crystal. The same shape as the peak of Figure 2 stands in every one of the 10 cells. Shifted by 36 degrees it coincides with itself, so nothing distinguishes one cell from another.

Smoothed at the resolution, it becomes the round red wave. This is what had looked like the base wave.

## What moves is a wave coarser than the resolution

Then what is moving? A wave coarser still than the resolution.

[Figure 5 here: note_fig5_coarse_wave_centre_phase_en.png]

Figure 5. The coarsest wave. Four curves with the position of the crest θ₀ (▼) moved from 90 to 126 degrees. The shaded band is the cell that contains θ₀.

The position θ₀ of the crest of the wave in Figure 5 can be moved continuously as far as the formula goes. But what can be read is only which cell θ₀ is in.

Add this coarse wave to the rigid crystal and look at the power (the square).

[Figure 6 here: note_fig6_power_tooth_jump_en.png]

Figure 6. The power of the rigid crystal plus the coarse wave. Top: θ₀ at 102 degrees. Bottom: 114 degrees. ▼ marks the highest tooth.

I moved θ₀ smoothly from 102 to 114 degrees. The highest tooth moves from the cell at 90 degrees to the cell at 126 degrees in a single step. It does not stop anywhere in between.

Things appear to move continuously because the position of the crest of a wave coarser than the resolution is moving. And that position, too, is in fact discretised by the graduation of the resolution.

## The observer's resolution is a gauge

Putting this together: the resolution is not a property of the wave but the graduation of a ruler brought in by the reader.

In physics, a gauge is a freedom whose choice does not change the relations that can be read. Whether the resolution is a gauge in that sense as well is decided by what stays unchanged when the resolution is changed.

Something did stay unchanged: the viewing angle.

In the previous paper I wrote that the relation between two parties is read through a shared viewing angle and the ratio of sizes. With the formula of this experiment, if two pulses are read at the same resolution, the viewing angle is fixed by the ratio of the two amplitudes alone.

　　tan α = A₂ / A₁

The resolution disappears when the ratio is taken. Changing the resolution does not change the ratio that can be read. What is conserved is the viewing angle; the resolution is a quantity on the side that changes.

## Exchanging the winding number and the phase

The order of a harmonic is a winding number: how many times the wave winds around the circle. The winding number and the phase are conjugate to each other, in the same way that frequency and wavelength are conjugate.

In mathematics, the circle and the integers are each other's duals (Pontryagin duality). The two stand on an equal footing, and mathematics does not decide which is placed on the side of observation. Our way of reading decides.

Have we not simply assumed that the space of winding numbers is the observable space and that the space of phases is hidden inside? What happens if we read it the other way: the winding number cannot be read, and only the phase can? The exchange of the upper and lower rows of Figure 3 is this.

The exchange exchanges the internal space and the observation space.

The conjugate of a closed space is the integers, that is, discrete values. If the resolution is finite, the operation that shifts the phase by one graduation and the operation that shifts the order by one give different results when their order is exchanged. This is a known formula called the finite Weyl relation, and its limit of infinitely fine graduation is the commutation relation of quantum mechanics. I think discreteness and quantumness may come out of this exchange.

## With quaternions, it becomes an exchange of 1 and 3

From here on is what became visible only with quaternions.

In the quaternion wave A e^{nθ}, n is a unit vector in three dimensions. With n held fixed, θ runs around one closed circle. The ladder of harmonics and the resolution are on the side of this circle. It has one component. The phase nθ, on the other hand, has three components.

So the exchange of winding number and phase becomes, for quaternions, an exchange of 1 and 3. Read in reverse, the readable side is the three-component phase, that is, the space of the right panel of Figure 1.

For complex numbers, n is fixed as the single i, and the phase too has one component. The exchange is 1 and 1, and its form does not change under the exchange. That is why I could not notice this exchange while thinking with complex numbers.

## The observable space is on the side that has been called the extra dimension

A hundred years ago, the theory of Kaluza and Klein placed a small closed circle inside, as an extra dimension, and placed the open, wide side in the observed space.

The present reading points the other way. The one closed circle is on the side of the order and of the ruler of resolution. The observed three-dimensional space is on the side of the three-component phase that has been called internal.

I also think that even if positions on the circle become discrete because of the resolution, the three-dimensional space should be observed as continuous. When rotations about axes of different orientation are composed, then, except when they close into a finite group, the places that can be reached grow without end and in general fill the sphere without a gap. The cases that close into a finite group are called the finite subgroups of SU(2), and Klein classified them in the nineteenth century. This has not yet been checked in the numerical experiment.

## All the parts are known; only the connecting map is missing

Every part this paper uses is already known.

- The sampling theorem (Shannon)
- The duality of the circle and the integers (Pontryagin)
- The finite Weyl relation (Weyl, Schwinger)
- Harmonic analysis on the three-dimensional sphere (the Peter–Weyl theorem)
- The classification of the finite subgroups of SU(2) (Klein)
- Niven's theorem and the Galois group of cyclotomic fields
- The McKay correspondence (between the finite subgroups and diagrams such as E₈)

The link with number theory enters here too. The number of graduations in one turn is an integer. But the projected value of a rotation by one graduation, cos(360°/M), is rational only when M is 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 (Niven's theorem). Clean integers appear, and yet mass ratios are not simple ratios of integers; I think this may be because of a map of this kind.

The necessary parts have already been found by mathematicians, beginning with Galois. Is it not only the map connecting them that is missing? That is the prediction of this paper.

There is also research in nearby directions: spacetime with an upper limit on its band, rational quantum mechanics that discretises Hilbert space, quantum mechanics built from finite groups alone, quantum reference frames that treat the choice of observer as a gauge, studies in which Planck's constant changes with resolution, and others. Table 2 of the paper lists 17 items and states where each agrees and where it differs. Within the range I surveyed, I did not find a reading that makes the resolution a gauge and the viewing angle the conserved quantity, nor a reading that exchanges the observation space and the internal space.

## The remaining questions

The biggest question remains.

Why does the viewing angle, which does not change when the resolution is changed, have a minimum value that does not depend on the observer? This minimal viewing angle is what corresponds to Planck's constant. Nothing in the present formulas gives a minimum.

The other is the identity of the wave coarser than the resolution. Whether it is a low-order component of the same pulse or a different wave is not yet known.

## What is shown and what is not

To repeat, this paper proves nothing.

What is certain as identities and theorems is only the part made of known pieces. The rigid crystal, the resolution, the exchange, the side of the extra dimension: these are readings. The origin of discreteness and quantumness, the conservation of the viewing angle, the continuity of three dimensions, the connection with number theory: these are hypotheses. Section 16 of the paper puts this classification in a table.

The ways this prediction can fail are written there too. If only readouts in which the viewing angle changes with the resolution can be built. If the side of the three-component phase also closes into a finite group. If the minimal viewing angle comes out only in a form that depends on the resolution or on the observer. If any of these happens, I will discard this reading.

I published it unfinished in order to pin the thinking down at one point. I hope it reaches people who are thinking about the same problem.
