# Was gravity inside quantum theory all along? — a hundred years of the imaginary unit and normalization

Noriaki Kihara  
2026-08-05

What I am about to write, read at face value, is pure crackpottery.

An amateur telling a hundred years of theoretical physics: you have been holding the thing you were searching for.

So let me make three declarations first.

**First. The Standard Model and general relativity are both exactly correct.** The reading I present here changes not a single prediction of the existing theories. Nowhere in this essay is there a claim that either one is wrong.

**Second. This is not a new theory; it is a proposal to re-read.** The same calculations, the same predictions, the same experimental results — with a different way of keeping the books. I call this a different mapping of the same results.

**Third. I will name the place where this can be tested.** If the re-reading is more than wordplay, there must be a place where it parts ways with the existing interpretation. I name it at the end.

Being able to make these three declarations is, I believe, the boundary line against crackpottery. A crank says physics is wrong. This essay says the opposite: **physics is doubly right — so right, on both sides, that the two halves cannot be joined. And that hundred-year irony may have an explanation.**

## A hundred years of struggle

Modern physics has two completed theories.

General relativity, which describes gravity. Quantum theory, which describes the microscopic world, and its culmination, the Standard Model.

Both are perfectly correct as far as they have been tested. And for a hundred years, the two have refused to be joined.

Quantize gravity naively and the calculations blow up to infinity. String theory built a magnificent mathematics complete with extra dimensions; the holographic principle re-projected the universe onto its boundary. All of it beautiful. None of it settled.

The greatest unsolved problem in physics: the unification of gravity and quantum theory.

So far, this is the textbook story.

From here on, it is a story about irony.

## 1921 — the prototype of the answer was already there

Rewind the history.

In 1921, a mathematician named Kaluza published a strange paper. Write Einstein's theory of gravity not in four dimensions but in five. Decompose the five-dimensional equations back into four, and alongside the gravitational field there appear components that behave exactly like the electromagnetic potential.

Later, Klein connected this to quantum theory: the momentum along the fifth, unseen direction is tied to electric charge.

Charge is momentum along an invisible direction.

As a unification of gravity and electromagnetism, it is about as geometrical and as beautiful as anything ever proposed. The theory had difficulties, fell out of the mainstream, and was shelved.

Remember this. The idea that **momentum along an invisible axis is the true identity of a force** had already reached the shape of a correct answer, a hundred years ago.

## 1926 — the imaginary unit walks in

Around the same time, quantum mechanics was born.

Schrödinger's wave function was complex-valued. The imaginary unit i had entered the heart of physics, seriously, for the first time.

Why must it be complex?

To this day there is no definitive answer. It was accepted as just how things are, and a hundred years passed.

In the reading of our wave model, the answer is this:

**i is the mark of the boundary between the axes you can observe and the axes you cannot.**

What does that mean? The next section opens the box.

## The reveal — a single equation

Our research series starts from a single condition.

The sum of the squares of all components is zero.

x² + y² + z² + t² + R² + Q² = 0

Six axes. The three directions of space, x, y, z. Time t. The scale of space (the radius of curvature) R. And the axis of charge, Q.

For ordinary numbers, a sum of squares vanishes only when every term is zero. For the condition to hold nontrivially, **some of the axes must be imaginary**.

So attach the imaginary unit to the unobservable axes — t, R, Q:

x² + y² + z² + (it)² + (iR)² + (iQ)² = 0

Rearranged:

x² + y² + z² − t² = R² + Q²

Look at the left side. It is **the spacetime interval of special relativity**. On a light ray the left side is zero. The Minkowski signature (+++−) has come out not as an assumption but from the zero-sum condition and the placement of the imaginary units.

Look at the right side. The sum of squares of the scale axis R and the charge axis Q. Write it as R′².

Here is what the equation says.

**In six dimensions, everything is light.** The six-axis sum of squares is always zero — everyone lives on the six-dimensional light cone.

**Whatever appears, in four dimensions, to sit off the light cone — whatever appears to have mass — has not lost anything. The invisible axes R and Q are carrying that budget as momentum.** The amount carried is R′.

Mass is momentum along R. Charge is momentum along Q. Energy is momentum along time. Ordinary momentum is momentum along space. **The quantities we have been calling conserved charges are all the same one momentum, pointing along different axes.**

And if mass is momentum along the scale axis —

**then gravity, the theory of distorted scale, has been contained in this equation from the start.**

## Then why was it invisible for a hundred years?

Here is the heart of the matter. Quantum theory did not lack this structure.

**It had it — and erased it, by a bookkeeping convention.**

Quantum mechanics has a procedure called normalization. The total magnitude of the wave function is always set to one. It is treated as a harmless convention, needed for the probability interpretation.

Seen through the six axes, normalization is not harmless. **It is the operation that erases, at every step, the overall scale of the wave function — the information of the R axis.**

At the moment of its birth, quantum theory threw away scale for the sake of probability. And the scale axis was precisely where gravity lives.

**Quantum theory fails to mesh with gravity not because it lacks gravity, but because it is the theory that deleted, with its own hands, the axis where gravity resides.**

Note what this does not say. It does not say the normalization was a mistake. The projection was done correctly — which is exactly why quantum theory is correct about everything on the remaining axes. Correct, and incomplete by one axis: that is the precise shape of the irony.

## What you throw away comes back

If this reading is right, the century that followed starts to make sense.

**Divergences and renormalization.** The calculations of quantum field theory blow up to infinity, and physicists tamed the infinities with a procedure called renormalization — a decades-long undertaking. Through the six-axis lens, a divergence is **the discarded scale axis pushing back from inside the calculation**. That the renormalization group — the technique of chasing a theory across changes of scale — works so magnificently is because scale is a real axis. It was the repayment, with interest, of a variable the theory had thrown away.

**Gauge theory.** The gauge symmetry at the heart of the Standard Model says that physics does not change when you rotate the phase of the wave function. Through the six-axis lens, this is **the rediscovery, under the name of internal symmetry, of the very axes that had been erased**. The word itself testifies. Gauge (Eichung) originally means calibration of a measuring scale, and when Weyl introduced it in 1918 it was literally a **transformation of scale** — an operation on the R axis. That attempt failed, and in 1929 it was reborn as a **transformation of phase** — an operation on the Q axis — and succeeded. From scale to phase. The trace of the erased axis survives, fossilized, inside the terminology.

**Kaluza–Klein.** The hundred-year-old answer — charge as momentum along an unseen direction — has its seat ready in the six axes, as the Q axis. Perhaps it was shelved not because the idea was wrong, but because the ledger was short by one more axis.

**String theory and holography.** Build extra dimensions; re-project onto a boundary. Both can be read as **going outside to search for something that was inside all along**. The thing being searched for had been living, since 1926, inside the imaginary unit.

## Our experiments have been walking on this reading

This is not just words. Each piece of the reading has been put through the net of verification, separately, in published numerical experiments and derivations.

That the zero-sum condition inherently contains quantumness (discreteness of states). That an inverse-square law is derived from a closed phase system with no force and no gravitational constant put in. That the equivalence principle comes out not as an assumption but as a corollary of a readout theorem. That only a single coherent wave — six-dimensional light — undergoes inflation-like runaway expansion, while a sea that already carries structure never inflates. That particles and antiparticles are produced in pairs, settling the ledger of conservation laws as they appear.

Each of these is public as an independent paper, with programs and raw data (links at the end). This essay is a single historical reading laid on top of those measurements.

## The place of testing

As promised, here is where this reading meets experiment.

A different mapping of the same results does not change the predictions of the existing theories, so most experiments cannot tell the two apart. But at the place where gravity and quantum superposition meet directly — experiments that ask **whether two bodies can become quantum-entangled through gravity alone** (a proposal known as the BMV experiment; technology is moving toward it) — the difference in bookkeeping may surface. Whether the entanglement requires an independent mediating degree of freedom, or appears as a correlation carried by the shared, erased axis, is exactly where a re-reading of gravity's address would first be forced to show its hand.

However this experiment turns out, I will follow the result. A re-reading does not outrank an experiment.

## In one line

**Perhaps gravity and quantum theory have resisted unification for a hundred years not because they are far apart, but because quantum theory, at birth, erased the scale axis for the sake of its probability ledger.**

The imaginary unit was the signature of the invisible axes. Normalization was the erasure of gravity. Renormalization was the repayment. Gauge was the rediscovery of the erased axis.

The thing being searched for was inside the equation all along.

— To repeat: this is a proposal for a re-reading, not a verified claim. But it is a fact that our entire experimental series has advanced in consistency with this reading. So I decided it was time to write the reading down, as a story about irony.
