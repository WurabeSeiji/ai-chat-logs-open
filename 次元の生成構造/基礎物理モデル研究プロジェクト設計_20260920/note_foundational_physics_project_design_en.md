# Design the Search Before Searching for the Answer

-- How I rebuilt my search for the foundations of physics as a single project

Noriaki Kihara

## Interesting discoveries were the biggest trap

For the past few years I have been running numerical experiments to see whether spacetime, particles, mass, charge, and time -- instead of being given from the start -- can arise naturally from less material: nameless "states" and the "relations" between them.

This way of searching has a trap.

Build a candidate equation, run it, and interesting phenomena appear one after another: singularities, closed structures, metastable states, exponential growth. Then the phenomena you found start to look as if they themselves were the goal of the research, and the direction of the work shifts every time.

An interesting numerical phenomenon and the foundational structure you are actually looking for are not the same thing.

## So I made the research itself the design object

The document published here is not a physics paper. No completed theory, no new equation appears in it.

What I wrote is the design document of a research project.

Exploratory projects, in which neither the goal nor the method can be fixed at the start, need management methods different from ordinary plan execution -- this has long been pointed out in project-management research. My own forty years of IT consulting and systems design say exactly the same thing.

So I redefined this research as the following iteration:

philosophical principles -> goal image -> preliminary design -> feasibility verification -> numerical experiments -> revision of the design and the goal image

The important part: the goal image itself is not fixed. If experiments or mathematical examination show it to be impossible, it is the goal that gets corrected.

## The starting point is nameless states

What exists at the beginning is kept to a minimum.

There is only one first-principle candidate: states exist that have no names, yet are mutually distinguishable and countable. That is all.

States are given no attributes in advance -- no electron or photon, no mass or charge, no position or time. A state cannot even be read directly; reading anything out requires interaction with other states. In other words, observation means readout accompanied by interaction.

What I hope will emerge from there -- a global conserved quantity, spontaneous extension in directions that look spatial, monotonic accumulation in a direction that looks temporal, particles as local condensations -- is written down not as axioms but as a provisional goal image, placed where verification can reject it.

## I also wrote down prohibitions for myself

In the middle of a search, there is a temptation to insert corrections just to get the result you want. So the document states the prohibitions explicitly:

- Do not give particle names to the initial states
- Do not fix spatial or temporal axes from outside
- Do not hide divergences with result-driven normalization or saturation functions
- Do not call a numerical phenomenon a discovery without checking it against known physics

And among the success conditions I wrote this: the discovery of infeasibility is also a research outcome. If you can prove that something does not work, that too is progress.

## The role of this document

This planning document is a map -- so that the coming exploration neither drifts nor gets fixed prematurely into a single theory.

The Standard Model and General Relativity are treated not as objects to replace, but as known successes that must ultimately be reproduced. Preserve not only what was discovered, but why that computation was performed. For an independent researcher sustaining a long search with limited resources, I believe this is the most important discipline.
