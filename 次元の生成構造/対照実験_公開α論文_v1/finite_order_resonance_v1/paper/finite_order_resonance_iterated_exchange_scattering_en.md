## Abstract

This study began with the explicit aim of testing whether an exchange-scattering model of a closed phase system could explain values associated with the low- and high-energy fine-structure constant. In the preceding study, the exchange-scattering coefficient $R$ was swept in System A, which reads localization transfer between wave packets, and System B, which reads a white-cat/black-cat/gray-cat metastable interface. Sharp peaks were observed near the regions corresponding to $\alpha^{-1}\approx137$ and $\alpha^{-1}\approx128$, but the reason why peaks formed at those positions remained unresolved.

This paper reports a reanalysis of the full-range sweep, root-centered precision sweeps, operator-eigenvalue analysis, and 50- and 80-digit calculations performed to identify the cause. The analysis shows that the observed peaks do not arise from the assumed electromagnetic-coupling readout alone. They are generated by exact recurrence of the two-channel exchange-scattering operator

$$
U_R=
\begin{pmatrix}
r&t\\
t&r
\end{pmatrix},
$$

whose antisymmetric eigenvalue returns to unity after a finite number of iterations:

$$
U_R^n=I.
$$

The finite-order roots are

$$
\boxed{
R_{n,m}=\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi m}{n}\right)
}.
$$

The principal low-energy peak is $R_{124,23}$, and the former high-energy principal candidate is $R_{122,23}$. A separate even-order root $R_{620,117}$ lies near the high-energy value $\alpha^{-1}(M_Z^2)=128.946\pm0.015$ and gives

$$
N(R_{620,117})
=\frac{4\pi}{(1-R_{620,117})^2}
=128.947864735670559.
$$

Its difference from the quoted value is $0.0018647$, or $0.1243\sigma$.

The 50-digit calculations produced depths near 52, while the 80-digit calculations produced depths from 82 to 83. At an exact finite-order root, the residual vanishes in ideal arithmetic, so the depth is formally infinite. This is not a divergence of energy or amplitude; it means that the resonance line becomes infinitely sharp.

The principal result is not only the analytic root formula. Two numerical models originally constructed for different purposes---wave-packet localization transfer and observation selection at a metastable two-state interface---share the same complex exchange-scattering kernel and reproduce operator-intrinsic finite-order resonance. Exact recurrence can therefore be tested as an iterated wave-packet interaction calculation rather than remaining only an abstract operator statement.

After this analysis, a literature search revealed connections to exact state-independent Floquet recurrence from cyclotomic spectra, Farey organization of resonance diagrams, the decomposition of $E_8$ into 31 orthogonal eight-dimensional subspaces, and studies relating exceptional algebras to the fine-structure constant. These works did not motivate the numerical experiment; they were identified only after the finite-order roots had been found.

We initially expected this numerical experiment to explain $\alpha$. What we directly found was a different finite-order resonance phenomenon connected to a generally known class of exact recurrences. It remains unresolved why such sharp finite-order roots occur near the two physical values of $\alpha^{-1}$. Determining whether this proximity is an arithmetic coincidence or follows from four-dimensional discrete geometry or another structural necessity is the central task of the next stage.

**Keywords:** fine-structure constant, exchange scattering, finite-order resonance, quantum recurrence, Floquet operator, wave-packet localization, metastable state, $E_8$, reproducible computation

# 1. Research Motivation

## 1.1 A Search Deliberately Aimed at the Fine-Structure Constant

The motivation of this study must not be obscured. We explicitly expected that the exchange-scattering structure of a closed phase system might explain a value corresponding to the fine-structure constant.

We define the exchange weight by

$$
G_R:=1-R
$$

and assume the correspondence

$$
G_R=e
$$

with the electromagnetic coupling amplitude in the standard theory,

$$
e=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha}.
$$

This is the correspondence hypothesis of the present study. We further define the readout

$$
N(R):=\alpha^{-1}.
$$

Since

$$
(1-R)^2=4\pi\alpha,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
N(R)=\frac{4\pi}{(1-R)^2}
}.
$$

Under this correspondence hypothesis, exchange-scattering coefficients can be assigned to the low-energy value

$$
\alpha^{-1}(0)=137.035999177(21)
$$

and the high-energy value

$$
\alpha^{-1}(M_Z^2)=128.946\pm0.015.
$$

Thus, the region $R\approx0.7$ was not selected accidentally. We examined whether the effective-response region found in the localization-transfer and metastable-interface experiments could connect to the exchange weight corresponding to the electromagnetic coupling amplitude.

## 1.2 System A: Wave-Packet Localization Transfer

The first starting point is a localization-exchange model in which an exchange-interference scattering matrix acts on wave-packet vectors [1,2]. A is a low-localization wave and B is a localized wave containing additional harmonics. We iterate

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
A'\\B'
\end{pmatrix}
=
U_R
\begin{pmatrix}
A\\B
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The readouts are

$$
L,\qquad N_{\mathrm{eff}},\qquad B_{\mathrm{to}A},
$$

where $L$ is localization, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective harmonic order, and $B_{\mathrm{to}A}$ measures the transfer of the initial B-side harmonic distribution into A.

At the endpoints of complete transmission $R=0$ and complete reflection $R=1$, the distinction between the low- and high-order waves was preserved. Under intermediate scattering, localization and harmonic structure were periodically transferred between the two channels. In particular, the localization and effective harmonic orders of the two channels approached one another near $R\approx0.70$.

This model was not originally designed to search for exact recurrence. It was constructed to numerically investigate an interaction in which a wave packet appears to become localized through exchange with another wave packet.

## 1.3 System B: White-Cat, Black-Cat, and Gray-Cat Metastable Interface

The second starting point is the white-cat/black-cat/gray-cat model, which reads the distribution and observation selection of two states A and B [3]. From complex amplitudes $a$ and $b$, define

$$
p_A=|a|^2,\qquad
p_B=|b|^2,\qquad
S=p_A-p_B.
$$

- $S\approx+1$: white-cat state A.
- $S\approx-1$: black-cat state B.
- $S\approx0$: gray state.

Among gray states, a state that fluctuates with small amplitude, survives a weak C readout, and is selected into A or B by a strong D observation is called a gray metastable phase.

This model was likewise not designed from the outset to reproduce the fine-structure constant or Floquet recurrence. It was constructed to distinguish a readout that does not destroy a metastable mixed state from strong observational selection.

## 1.4 The Question Left by the Previous Paper

The paper of July 15, 2026 [4] connected Systems A and B through their common exchange-scattering coefficient $R$. It found that:

- with an additional harmonic, the response concentrates in the region corresponding to 137;
- the candidate position barely moves when the harmonic type, amplitude, phase, or wavelength is changed; and
- the full System B sweep contains deep peaks near the regions read as 137 and 128.

The following question, however, remained unresolved:

> Why do sharp peaks form at those positions?

This paper reports the investigation undertaken to answer that question and the resulting identification of the cause.

# 2. Computational Model and Readout

## 2.1 Exchange-Scattering Coefficients

For a reflection probability $R\in[0,1]$, define

$$
\theta(R):=\arcsin\sqrt R.
$$

The transmission and reflection amplitudes are

$$
t=e^{i\theta}\cos\theta,
\qquad
r=-ie^{i\theta}\sin\theta.
$$

Therefore,

$$
|t|^2=1-R,
\qquad
|r|^2=R.
$$

## 2.2 Two-Channel Exchange Operator

One scattering iteration is defined by

$$
\boxed{
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{j+1}\\
B_{j+1}
\end{pmatrix}
=
U_R
\begin{pmatrix}
A_j\\
B_j
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U_R=
\begin{pmatrix}
r&t\\
t&r
\end{pmatrix}
}.
$$

Because the coefficients satisfy

$$
|r|^2+|t|^2=1,
\qquad
r^*t+t^*r=0,
$$

we have

$$
U_R^\dagger U_R=I.
$$

Thus, the exact fixed-$R$ map is unitary and does not dissipatively attract the state norm to a point.

The ``concentration'' at a peak in this study is not dissipative contraction in state space. It is resonance selection in $R$-parameter space, where a closed-orbit condition concentrates at particular values. The increase of wave-packet localization observed in System A is a redistribution of localization by unitary interference, not contraction of the total norm.

## 2.3 Initial State of System B

Let

$$
A_0=\sqrt{\frac12+s_0},
\qquad
B_0=\sqrt{\frac12-s_0}\,e^{i\phi}.
$$

The principal sweep uses

$$
s_0=0.01,\qquad \phi=0.
$$

The initial population difference is therefore

$$
S_0=|A_0|^2-|B_0|^2=0.02.
$$

## 2.4 Gray Error and Depth

At each time, define the population difference by

$$
S_j=
\frac{|A_j|^2-|B_j|^2}
{|A_j|^2+|B_j|^2}.
$$

For a prefix of length $k$, define

$$
\overline S_k
=\frac1k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}S_j,
$$

$$
A_k
=\frac{\max_{j<k}S_j-\min_{j<k}S_j}{2},
$$

and

$$
D_k
=\left|
\overline S_{k,\mathrm{second}}
-\overline S_{k,\mathrm{first}}
\right|.
$$

The gray error is

$$
\varepsilon_k
=|\overline S_k|
+|A_k-0.02|
+D_k,
$$

and the depth is

$$
\boxed{
d_k=-\log_{10}\varepsilon_k
}.
$$

A periodic orbit becomes a deeper peak when its mean approaches zero, its amplitude approaches $0.02$, and the drift between the first and second halves approaches zero.

## 5.2 The Theoretical Limit

At a root whose relatively prime representation $(m,n)$ has even fundamental order $n$, reading two complete periods $2n$ gives exactly

$$
\overline S=0,
\qquad
A=0.02,
\qquad
D=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\varepsilon=0,
$$

and

$$
d=-\log_{10}\varepsilon
\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

The diverging quantity is gray depth, not wave energy or amplitude. The height at the peak center increases with precision while the width narrows. A finite-precision measurement or coarse $R$ sweep can therefore miss the peak easily.

# 6. Mathematical Origin of the Peaks

## 6.1 Symmetric and Antisymmetric Decomposition

Decompose the two-channel state as

$$
X_j:=\frac{A_j+B_j}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
Y_j:=\frac{A_j-B_j}{\sqrt2}.
$$

The eigenvalues of the exchange operator are

$$
\lambda_s=r+t=1,
$$

and

$$
\lambda_a=r-t=-e^{2i\theta}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
X_j=X_0,
\qquad
Y_j=\lambda_a^jY_0.
$$

With the projection operators

$$
P_s=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\1&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P_a=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1\\-1&1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
U_R^j=P_s+\lambda_a^jP_a
}.
$$

## 6.2 Derivation of the Finite-Order Roots

Let

$$
R_{n,m}
=\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi m}{n}\right),
\qquad
1\le m<\frac n2,
\qquad
\gcd(m,n)=1.
$$

Then

$$
\theta
=\arcsin\sqrt{R_{n,m}}
=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi m}{n},
$$

so

$$
\lambda_a
=-e^{2i\theta}
=e^{-2\pi i m/n}.
$$

It follows that

$$
\lambda_a^n=1
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
U_{R_{n,m}}^n=I
}.
$$

This is not a numerical fit. It is an analytic consequence derived directly from the scattering coefficients used in the current program.

## 6.3 Closed Form of the System B Time Series

The population difference is

$$
S_j=
\frac{2\operatorname{Re}
\left(
\langle X_0,Y_0\rangle\lambda_a^j
\right)}
{\|X_0\|^2+\|Y_0\|^2}.
$$

Under the principal condition $\phi=0$ and $s_0=0.01$,

$$
\frac{2\langle X_0,Y_0\rangle}
{\|X_0\|^2+\|Y_0\|^2}
=0.02,
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{
S_j
=0.02\cos\left[
j\left(
\pi+2\arcsin\sqrt R
\right)
\right]
}.
$$

This equation shows that the iterated scattering in the original program and the Chebyshev recurrence used for the multiprecision calculation evaluate the same time series.

## 6.4 Low Sensitivity to Initial Values

For a general initial state,

$$
S_j=C\cos(j\omega+\varphi_0),
\qquad
\omega=\arg\lambda_a.
$$

The amplitude $C$ and initial phase $\varphi_0$ depend on the initial state. However,

$$
\omega(R)=\pi+2\arcsin\sqrt R
$$

is determined by the operator alone. Consequently, changing the initial amplitude, initial phase, wave-packet shape, or harmonic content does not move

$$
R_{n,m}.
$$

What changes is the peak height, background, and visibility.

## 6.5 The Numbers 31, 124, and 248

At $R_{124,23}$,

$$
\lambda_a^{31}=i,
\qquad
\lambda_a^{62}=-1,
\qquad
\lambda_a^{93}=-i,
\qquad
\lambda_a^{124}=1.
$$

Thus,

$$
31\times4=124
$$

is one complete operator period, and

$$
124\times2=248
$$

is the number of samples used by the gray metric to read two complete periods.

The number 248 was not inserted into the scattering coefficients. It appeared as the time window that reads the 124-cycle closed orbit twice.

# 7. Program Structure

## 7.1 From Reflection Probability to Complex Scattering Amplitudes

The following excerpt is the core of the minimal System B implementation v5:

```python
def scattering_coefficients(reflection_rate: float):
    if reflection_rate < 0.0 or reflection_rate > 1.0:
        raise ValueError("reflection rate must be in [0, 1]")

    delta_f = 2.0 * math.asin(math.sqrt(reflection_rate))
    half_delta = 0.5 * delta_f
    phase = complex(math.cos(half_delta), math.sin(half_delta))

    t = phase * math.cos(half_delta)
    r = -1j * phase * math.sin(half_delta)
    return complex(t), complex(r), abs(t) ** 2, abs(r) ** 2
```

The only input to this function is $R$. Neither 137, 128, $\alpha$, $N(R)$, nor a finite-order root enters the calculation of the scattering amplitudes.

## 7.3 Gray Error

The evaluation function is

def prefix_error(prefix_sums, s_min, s_max):
    n = len(prefix_sums) - 1
    total = prefix_sums[n]
    s_mean = total / n
    s_amp = 0.5 * (s_max - s_min)

    mid = n // 2
    first_mean = prefix_sums[mid] / mid
    second_mean = (total - prefix_sums[mid]) / (n - mid)
    s_drift = abs(second_mean - first_mean)

    gray_error = (
        abs(s_mean)
        + abs(s_amp - 0.02)
        + s_drift
    )
    return gray_error, s_mean, s_amp, s_drift

## 7.7 Collision Trigger and the Scope of Iterated Scattering

The complete interaction separates into two stages:

1. The A and B wave packets approach within one cell, triggering the first scattering event.
2. The post-scattering outputs $A',B'$ are updated into the next cycle of iterated scattering.

The current System A and System B programs directly implement the second stage. They do not implement the trajectories by which wave packets approach through space until the first-collision condition is satisfied.

The result of this paper is therefore not a completed model of the entire interaction process. It is the identification that **the iterated exchange-scattering kernel after collision onset is sufficient to generate finite-order resonance**.

# 9. The Two Physical Models and Resonance Visibility

## 9.1 Harmonics Do Not Create the Roots; They Make the Roots Visible

Let the internal degrees of freedom of a wave packet be $\mathcal H$. Where the same exchange law acts on every internal component,

$$
U_R^{\mathrm{total}}
=U_R\otimes I_{\mathcal H}.
$$

The harmonic type, amplitude, phase, and wavelength change the initial vector in $\mathcal H$, but they do not change the two-channel eigenvalues

$$
1,\qquad -e^{2i\theta}.
$$

Additional harmonics are therefore not the cause that creates the finite-order roots. They are degrees of freedom that couple the antisymmetric eigensector to localization or population-difference observables and thereby make the resonance visible.

The exact conditions under which the resonance does not appear in the System B population difference are

$$
Y_0=0
$$

or

$$
\langle X_0,Y_0\rangle=0.
$$

In these cases, the antisymmetric eigenphase exists in the operator but is absent from the selected readout.

## 9.2 Why Different Models Read the Same Region

System A reads localization, harmonic order, and transfer ratio through

$$
\mathcal O_A(U_R^j\Psi_0).
$$

System B reads the A/B population difference, gray error, and metastable depth through

$$
\mathcal O_B(U_R^j\Psi_0).
$$

The observation functions differ, but the time-development operator is the same. The different physical models respond in the same $R$ region because their common exchange-scattering kernel has the same eigenphase.

This is the point of contact with physics in the present study. We did not begin by enumerating abstract cyclotomic roots. Experimental code designed to investigate wave-packet localization transfer and metastable selection detected the same operator resonance.

# 10. Connections to Prior Work Recognized after the Discovery

## 10.1 Chronology of the Literature Search

The works discussed below did not guide the full-range sweep or the program design. We searched the literature only after identifying $R_{124,23}$, $R_{122,23}$, and $R_{620,117}$ through numerical calculation and eigenvalue analysis.

This chronology is stated explicitly for two reasons:

1. to show that the numerical experiment was not a reproduction obtained by embedding known cyclotomic roots or an $E_8$ decomposition in the code; and
2. to distinguish what is known as a general phenomenon from the mathematical model and numerical discovery specific to this study.

## 10.2 Exact State-Independent Quantum Recurrence

Anand et al. classified exact state-independent recurrence times from the cyclotomic spectrum of finite-dimensional Floquet unitary operators [5]. The present relation

$$
U_R^n=I
$$

is a concrete two-channel instance connected to this general class of exact recurrence.

We therefore do not claim to have discovered the general concept of a finite-order unitary operator. The contribution is that a wave-packet numerical experiment aimed at the fine-structure constant independently reached this structure and detected it in both localization-transfer and metastable-interface models.

## 10.3 Farey Sequences and Resonance Organization

Tomás described the organization of physical resonance diagrams by Farey sequences of rational numbers [6]. The present roots are classified by

$$
\frac{m}{n}.
$$

Farey adjacency can therefore organize the closely spaced high-order roots on the high-energy side. That work, however, does not treat the exchange-scattering map, the fine-structure-constant correspondence, localization transfer, or the cat model used here.

## 10.4 The $31\times8$ Structure of $E_8$

After obtaining

$$
124=31\times4,
\qquad
248=31\times4\times2,
$$

we investigated a possible connection to the 248 dimensions of $E_8$ and its decomposition into 31 orthogonal eight-dimensional subspaces.

Thompson and later work treat structures associated with a decomposition of $E_8$ into 31 orthogonal eight-dimensional Cartan subalgebras [7,8]. The $E_8$ root system also separates into 112 integer-type roots and 128 half-integer-type roots [9].

These connections were noticed only after the finite-order-root discovery of July 18, 2026. The numbers 31, 128, and 248 were not selected in advance to tune the iteration count or exchange coefficient.

At present, no explicit map has been constructed from

$$
31\text{ time sectors}\times
4\text{ phases}\times
2\text{ channels}
$$

to the 31 eight-dimensional subspaces of $E_8$. The $E_8$ connection is therefore retained as a structural hypothesis.

## 10.5 Exceptional Algebras and the Fine-Structure Constant

El Naschie proposed a derivation connecting the $8\times31=248$ structure of $E_8$ to the fine-structure constant [10]. Singh discussed $1/137$ using octonionic spacetime and an exceptional Jordan algebra [11].

Neither line of work was the starting point of this study. We deliberately searched for the fine-structure constant, but did not use $E_8$ or an exceptional algebra to design the numerical peaks. Their relation to the present result was investigated only after obtaining the finite-order roots and the $31\times4\times2$ structure.

# 11. Discussion

## 11.1 What Did We Discover?

We initially expected that a closed exchange system could directly explain the fine-structure constant.

The numerical experiments did return sharp peaks near 137 and 128. Their direct cause, however, was not the assumed electromagnetic-coupling correspondence

$$
G_R=e
$$

itself. The direct cause is the finite-order exchange resonance

$$
\lambda_a^n=1.
$$

Thus, this study has not completed a direct derivation of $\alpha$. In the course of searching for the fine-structure constant, it discovered that the iterated exchange-scattering program detects exact finite-order recurrence.

Because general exact recurrence is present in prior work, the general phenomenon is a rediscovery of a different phenomenon. The following points are nevertheless specific to this study:

- it began from a wave-packet localization-transfer model;
- it also began from a weak-readout/strong-selection model of two metastable states;
- both models share the same exchange-scattering kernel;
- multiple even-order roots appear near the physical readout regions 137 and 128; and
- source code, raw data, full-range figures, local figures, and multiprecision results are preserved in reproducible form.

## 11.2 Why Are the Roots Near $\alpha$?

This is the most important question remaining after the result of this paper.

On the low-energy side,

$$
N(R_{124,23})
=137.036042914598545\ldots
$$

is close to

$$
\alpha^{-1}(0)
=137.035999177(21).
$$

On the high-energy side,

$$
N(R_{620,117})
=128.947864735670559\ldots
$$

is close to

$$
\alpha^{-1}(M_Z^2)
=128.946\pm0.015.
$$

There are countably infinitely many finite-order roots

$$
R_{n,m}=\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi m}{n}\right).
$$

Their arithmetic density can place high-order roots near an arbitrary real value and may account for part of the proximity.

However, this study did not merely select arbitrary roots after the fact:

- the original full-range sweep produced $R_{124,23}$ and $R_{122,23}$ as its principal peaks;
- the low-energy root has the short $31\times4$ temporal structure;
- an even root $R_{620,117}$ with infinite depth under the present gray metric lies near the high-energy observed value; and
- different wave-packet models responded to the same exchange-scattering region.

The proximity therefore cannot yet be declared either a mere coincidence or a physical necessity.

## 11.3 The Central Open Problem

The central open problem of this paper is stated without ambiguity:

> **We began this search expecting that a closed exchange system could explain the fine-structure constant $\alpha$. Within what has been established by the present analysis, however, we did not derive $\alpha$ itself; we merely rediscovered a different finite-order resonance phenomenon connected to the general theory of exact recurrence. Yet the extremely sharp peaks of that finite-order resonance actually occur near the two physical low- and high-energy values of $\alpha^{-1}$. Why this proximity occurs remains unexplained. Is it an accidental consequence of arithmetic density, or is it another necessity imposed by four-dimensional discrete geometry, $E_8$-type symmetry, a physical selection rule of exchange scattering, or an as-yet-unidentified structure? Distinguishing these alternatives is the central task of future work.**

The required discriminating calculations are:

1. enumerate all roots below a fixed maximum order $n_{\max}$;
2. calculate how often an even-order root at least as close to the observed value occurs near randomized reference values;
3. test whether localization, entropy, phase correlation, and transfer observables select the same roots independently of the gray metric;
4. randomize the initial state, harmonics, and time window to separate root location from visibility;
5. construct an explicit map connecting the four-dimensional 137-cell count, the 128 outer sector, the $31\times8$ decomposition, and the exchange operator; and
6. implement the same $U_R$ in a physical optical coupler, discrete-time quantum walk, or two-level Floquet system.

The fine-structure constant itself remains unexplained. A new question has been isolated: why do extremely sharp finite-order roots occur near the two physical values of $\alpha^{-1}$?
