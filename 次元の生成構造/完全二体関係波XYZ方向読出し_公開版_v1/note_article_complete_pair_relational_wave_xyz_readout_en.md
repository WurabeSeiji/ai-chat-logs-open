# If We Add More Waves, Do We Get More Spatial Dimensions?

Can quantities resembling position and acceleration be read from phase relations alone, without assuming a background space in advance?

In earlier experiments, I confirmed that such readouts could be constructed from the phase relation between two waves, A and B.

What happens, then, when the number of waves is increased to three or four? How many spatial directions emerge?

At first glance, three waves might seem to produce three dimensions, and four waves four dimensions.

The numerical experiments, however, gave a different result.

## What mattered was not the number of waves, but the number of pairwise relations

With two waves, A and B, there is one pairwise relation: AB.

With three waves, A, B, and C, there are three pairwise relations: AB, BC, and CA.

With four waves, A, B, C, and D, there are six relations: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, and CD.

Does this mean that ABC produces three-dimensional space and ABCD produces six-dimensional space?

The answer was again no.

## In ABC, three directions emerge, but only two can be read independently

The ABC system contains three relation waves: AB, BC, and CA.

In the numerical experiment, all three relation waves were active. In other words, three relational directions actually existed within the system.

However, only two directions could be read independently as phase relations.

The remaining direction was determined as the normal to the plane formed by the two readable directions.

In a spatial interpretation, the two independently readable directions form an XY plane, and the normal to that plane determines Z.

Thus, ABC produces three directions that can be interpreted as XYZ. Nevertheless, only the X and Y directions are directly readable from independent phase relations. Z is a dependent normal direction determined by those two directions.

## In ABCD, the six relations separated into three rotation planes

ABCD contains six pairwise relations.

If every relation direction were interpreted directly as an independent spatial axis, the system would appear to produce six-dimensional space.

The numerical experiment did not show that. Instead, the six relation directions separated into three rotation planes.

Only three spatial directions, corresponding to XYZ, could be read uniquely. The remaining three internal directions could not be assigned unique spatial directions.

The number of relations therefore grows from one to three and then to six, but the number of observable spatial directions does not grow at the same rate.

Internal relation directions continue to increase, while the uniquely readable spatial directions saturate at the three directions XYZ.

## What happens with five or more waves?

The numerical experiments reported here directly tested AB, ABC, and ABCD.

ABCDE and larger systems have not yet been tested numerically under the same conditions.

If the structure found here continues, however, adding more waves will not create new uniquely readable spatial directions.

Instead, it is expected to add internal relations whose directions cannot be determined uniquely, while the spatial readout remains limited to the three directions XYZ.

This is a theoretical interpretation of the present result. It is kept separate from what was directly confirmed by the numerical experiments.

## No background XYZ axes were assumed

The experiment did not begin with a background space containing predefined X, Y, and Z axes.

Nor were C and D introduced as observers or measuring devices. Every pairwise relation was treated as an equally ranked state wave.

The program also did not normalize the state at every step to preserve the result, and no observational damping was used.

Even so, quadratic closure, the total state magnitude, and covariance under relabeling were preserved within numerical precision.

## This does not yet explain why physical space has three dimensions

This result alone does not establish why the space we inhabit has exactly three dimensions.

It does show, however, that when we begin with waves and their relations rather than a predefined background space, the number of internal relations continues to grow while the readable spatial directions saturate at XYZ.

Whether this structure is genuinely connected to the three-dimensionality of observed space remains an open question.

Even so, the experiment provides a concrete result for a different starting question: instead of assuming from the outset that space is three-dimensional, how many spatial directions can actually be read from phase relations in a system with no background space?
