# A universe cannot begin from noise — only a clear wave on the verge of falling can ignite inflation

Noriaki Kihara  
2026-08-05

In the previous article, I reported this:

Inflation happened without a seed.

Even without planting a fluctuation by hand, our wave model started a rapid expansion by itself, created three directions, and stopped.

Then a natural question arises.

**Does inflation happen no matter what kind of wave you start from?**

This time, we answer that question by experiment.

To state the conclusion first: the answer was no.

And it was a no pointing in the opposite direction from what we expected.

## It began with a naive question

All the experiments so far had used the same starting point.

A state in which every wave rides exactly on one single oscillation. Imagine an orchestra in which every player sustains the same single note without the slightest deviation. A perfectly coherent wave, clear as a laser.

But think about it.

Isn't it rather unnatural to suppose that the universe began in such a well-behaved state?

A hubbub in which sounds of every pitch are mixed together — the state physicists call white noise — seems a far more natural picture of a beginning.

So we made a prediction.

**Starting from a sea of noise, the same inflation should occur.**

And we built an experiment to check it.

## Building the sea of noise, with no cheating

The preparation required one caution.

The waves of this model must satisfy the closure condition — the rule that the sum of all the squares equals zero.

When building the noise, we keep only this condition and leave everything else to the dice. If we shaped the waves by hand, it would not be an experiment.

We designed a construction in which the condition holds automatically, starting from random numbers.

Interestingly, a small theorem fell out of this design.

The DC component — the zeroth component of a wave — can never satisfy the condition on its own, no matter what. In this model, the DC component is denied the qualification to exist from the outset. Its removal was not a human choice; it was a consequence of the axiom.

In this way we prepared 82 initial states born from noise.

And we ran them on exactly the same stage as the traditional series that starts from a single clear wave.

## The result — not one of them expanded

Our prediction failed completely.

Figure 1. The vertical axis shows, on a logarithmic scale, how far the state has moved from its starting point. Gray is the series starting from a single clear wave: a long stillness, then a straight-line surge across 18 orders of magnitude — this is the inflation. Blue is the collection of waves extracted from the noise. Their stillness lasts zero steps; within the first few steps they slip loose and simply settle down. There is no surge anywhere.

The series starting from the clear wave showed, as always, a long runway followed by an 18-decade geometric surge.

But the 82 noise-born states, **without a single exception, did not expand.**

It is not that the surge arrives late. The surge as a process simply does not exist. A noise wave changes its shape smoothly within the first few steps, and then quietly settles.

Even more surprising was where they settled. The final state was the same one that the clear wave reaches at the end of its rapid expansion: that metastable state with three directions.

So the destination is shared. Only the road differs.

**The trace of an 18-decade surge is a fossil, showing that the system began from a single clear wave.**

## Why — a pencil balanced on its tip

Why does only the clear wave expand?

The key turned out to be balance.

The state in which everything rides one clear wave is, within this dynamics, a perfectly balanced state — in technical terms, a relative equilibrium.

Picture a pencil standing upside down on the tip of its lead.

As long as it stands perfectly, the pencil does not move. This is the identity of the long stillness before the surge — the runway. It looks still not because it is resting, but because it is standing at a balance on the verge of falling.

And once the balance breaks by even the slightest amount, the tilt grows in a doubling game. Exponential collapse. This is the identity of the 18-decade surge.

What about the noise, then?

The noise is not standing in the first place. It is already lying on the floor from the start. Something already lying down has no dramatic moment of falling. That is why it has no surge.

We did not leave this explanation as a metaphor. Three verification experiments turned it into numbers.

- Measuring, for every state, a quantity that directly tests whether it is standing (the misfit after advancing one step): for clear waves it appears in the 15th decimal place; for noise, in the 2nd. A separation of 13 orders of magnitude, with nothing in between
- Computing the falling rate from the dynamics: for the five-body system it agreed with the measured surge rate to five decimal places
- And there was a decisive case. Among the clear waves there was exactly one exception that did not expand. When we examined it, it was not on the verge of falling — it was **standing stably**. It cannot fall, so it does not expand. Even the exception obeyed the explanation

In summary, the fate of a wave falls into three classes.

- A wave lying on the floor (noise) unravels immediately
- A wave standing stably does nothing
- **Only a wave standing on the verge of falling collapses exponentially after a long stillness — and this is inflation**

## A bonus harvest — we measured the identity of the seed

In the previous article, I reported that inflation happens even without a seed.

But a pencil standing in perfect balance should never fall. Something must be giving the first tiny push.

This time, we ran an experiment to measure what that something is.

We deliberately added an artificial push and made it smaller and smaller; the time until the fall grows in a regular way.

(Insert Figure 2 here)

![Making the push smaller delays the fall linearly, until it hits a ceiling at a certain strength](第8論文_二段階seed除去による準安定相の因果分離/paper8_onset_mechanism_v1/fig_onset_eps_sweep_N00005.png)

Figure 2. The horizontal axis is the smallness of the push; the vertical axis is the time until the fall. The points lie on a clean straight line. The slope of the line agreed with the theoretical prediction to 4 parts in 1000. Now look at the right end. Once the push becomes smaller than a certain strength, the time stops growing.

The reason the growth stops is this.

The artificial push has become smaller than the push the system carries on its own.

Reading that intrinsic push off the straight line, its strength sits in the 13th decimal place.

And this value matched, digit for digit in its order of magnitude, the assembly error that inevitably remains when the clear wave is built inside a computer — the last tiny tilt that persists no matter how hard one aims at perfect balance.

**The identity of the seed is the remainder left by aiming at perfection and falling just short of it.**

A perfect equilibrium cannot be built, in principle or in practice. A construction residue always remains. That remainder grows, doubling and doubling, and in time becomes a universe-scale expansion.

This is the answer to the mystery left last time — why does the surge begin after the runway?

## It turned out to be a forty-year-old dispute

To be honest, this experiment was not designed after a study of prior work.

We started from the naive expectation that noise should be more natural, the experiment rejected that expectation, and, faced with the unexpected result, we went back and re-surveyed the cosmology literature.

What we found was startling.

**Inflationary cosmology has a forty-year-old unsolved issue called the initial conditions problem.**

Standard inflation theory assumes, as its starting state, a nearly uniform coherent field — precisely a single clear wave. It does not derive this; it assumes it. And theorists have long been split over the legitimacy of that assumption — to the point that the participants themselves call it a schism.

There are numerical-relativity groups claiming that inflation ignites even from chaos. Read closely, however, their initial data first restrict the field values to a convenient range before adding disorder. In our language, that is not noise; it is a clear wave with fluctuations laid on top.

Without knowing about this dispute, we ran into the same question inside our own model, and obtained a definite answer there.

From noise, no amplification comes. Only from a clear wave on the verge of falling.

Of course, our model is not a theory of the real universe. It contains no spacetime metric, no gravity, no field theory — only relations between waves. So we cannot brandish this result as a proof about the actual cosmos.

Still, a model built on minimal axioms reached, by its own independent route, the same question as a forty-year-old controversy, and returned a clear answer within its own walls. That convergence, we believe, is worth putting on record.

## In one line

Last time, we showed that

**inflation-like rapid expansion happens by itself, without assuming a fluctuation.**

This time, we questioned the starting point itself, and showed that

**only a single clear wave standing at a falling balance can ignite the expansion, and a sea of noise never inflates,**

and that

**the identity of the seed is the unavoidable remainder of an assembly that aims at perfect equilibrium.**

Three mysteries remain, once again.

Which clear waves stand on the verge of falling, and which stand stably? Can this be predicted from the shape of the wave?

How does a single clear wave arise out of a sea of noise?

And how is matter born from the sea after the fall? This third one is already in motion, in a separate line of experiments.
