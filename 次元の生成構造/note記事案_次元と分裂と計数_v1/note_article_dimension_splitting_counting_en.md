# Why Do Directions Stop at Three, While Waves Multiply on Their Own?

In the previous article, "If We Add More Waves, Do We Get More Spatial Dimensions?", I reported a surprising result.

Increasing the number of waves from two to three to four, the directions that could be uniquely read out as spatial directions stopped at three — XYZ. The internal relations kept multiplying, yet the readable directions saturated at three.

But that experiment went up to four bodies only. And several questions remained.

If we increase the number of bodies much further, surely the directions must eventually increase?

Where do all the overflowing relation waves go?

And is a wave even stable in the first place? Why is there not just one particle-like lump of waves, but many?

I investigated this continuation in four papers. And the results went beyond what I expected.

Waves are not stable. A single wave — in a manner reminiscent of inflation in the early universe — amplified exponentially from a nearly zero seed far below any measurement, split on its own, and then settled into a quasi-stable state.

Let me report in order.

## Even at twelve bodies, the directions stayed at three

First, I increased the number of bodies to twelve.

The number of relation waves grows explosively: three waves for three bodies, six for four bodies, fifteen for six bodies, sixty-six for twelve bodies. It grows as the square of the number of bodies.

Counting the rotational degrees of freedom that can move simultaneously, however, gives growth only in proportion to the number of bodies — distinctly slower than the growth of the relation waves.

And the directions uniquely readable as spatial directions remained three.

So this system has three layers. The number of relations explodes quadratically. The rotational degrees of freedom grow only linearly. The readable directions are fixed at three. The more you add, the wider the gaps between the three layers become.

## The overflowing relation waves organize into rotation planes

Are the many unreadable relation waves, then, just mixed together in disorder?

They are not.

Examining the motion of the system, the many relation waves were organized into a small number of rotation planes. The overall rotation decomposes into a superposition of several mutually independent planar rotations.

What matters most is that the content of each single plane has the same form as the very first thing this series studied: the relation between two waves (AB).

The AB unit examined in the smallest experiment reappears, several times over, as a component inside the larger system. Readouts of distance-like quantities could be reproduced plane by plane in the same way.

Here, for the first time, several particle-like units appear inside one and the same system.

## A single wave splits on its own

The next question is whether such a plane is stable.

Prepare a state in which only one rotation plane is moving and the remaining components are dormant at nearly zero. This is a state in which only a single wave exists.

In the experiments so far, the way of rotating (the generator) was fixed at the start. Now relax just this one condition: rebuild the way of rotating, at every step, from the current state. In other words, strengthen by one notch the degree to which the system references itself.

Then the single wave split spontaneously.

The dormant component starts below one part in ten billion in energy ratio — far too small to measure. It was amplified by the same factor every step, climbed straight up through the orders of magnitude, and took hold at more than ten percent of the whole. A new wave was born.

This is no peculiarity of small systems. Increasing the number of bodies to three hundred (44,850 relation waves) and then to one thousand (499,500 relation waves), and lowering the seed to ten to the minus thirtieth power in energy ratio, the same curve appeared. It climbs straight through more than twenty orders of magnitude at a constant factor. The curves for three hundred bodies and one thousand bodies almost coincide: the speed of amplification barely changes as the number of bodies grows.

This curve has an important property concerning scale. The vertical axis is a fraction, and the axioms of this system give no meaning to the absolute size of amplitudes. Only ratios act as physics. And in the measurements, the arrival time at the ceiling simply grew in proportion to the number of digits of the seed (roughly seventy to ninety-five steps per order of magnitude). So even starting from a far deeper seed, say ten to the minus thirty-eighth power, we can expect the same straight line to simply grow longer, with the shape of the curve unchanged. The present measurements confirmed this expectation directly, down to a depth of thirty orders of magnitude.

Making the triggering seed ten thousand times smaller only delays the onset time; the splitting always occurs. Even setting the seed exactly to zero, the same splitting rose at the same speed, from nothing but the minuscule fluctuations of the computer's rounding errors.

So splitting is not caused by the size of the trigger. The single-wave state itself is unstable, and the system picks up and amplifies any fluctuation, however small. Splitting is not an accident; it is a property of the system.

The comparison to inflation at the opening referred to this structure: a nearly zero seed, amplified exponentially at a rate independent of the seed's size, with a new structure taking hold as the growth saturates — the skeleton of exponential expansion followed by the appearance of structure at its end. To be clear, this is not the stage at which any correspondence between this model and cosmology can be claimed. It is an analogy, nothing more.

## The fates of splitting divide into three

What happens to the system after it splits? Tracking up to twenty thousand steps, the fates divided into three classes.

- Expansion: the new wave takes hold, and the allocation spreads across the whole system
- Bounded mixing: the allocation spreads, but the new wave never quite takes hold
- Recurrence: the system keeps returning, deeply and repeatedly, toward the original wave

What decides the class is, first, the number of bodies. With four bodies or fewer the new wave cannot take hold; with five or more, it took hold for every initial condition examined. The boundary lies between four and five bodies.

Furthermore, the value of a certain quantity the system conserves exactly (the conserved value of the closure) also selects the destination. At the same number of bodies, the stratum where this conserved value is zero and the stratum where it is large end up in different classes.

In systems of five or more bodies, a single wave always divides into many waves. The birth of many particle-like units is not the exception in this system — it is the standard outcome.

## So how many waves are there? — In fact, they cannot be counted

Up to here I have used the phrase "the number of waves" as if it were obvious. The last paper examines the ground under that phrase.

The conclusion was unexpected: the number of waves is not determined inside the system.

Think of the sound of a single string. Whether you count it as one sound, or as ten components in a superposition of the fundamental and its harmonics, is decided not by the string but by how finely the listener resolves what they hear — by the resolution.

The waves of this system are the same. One and the same closed state can be legitimately rewritten as a one-term expression, a two-term expression, an eight-term expression — as many terms as you like. It can be proven that no mechanism exists inside the system that decides which way of counting is correct. The number of waves is a resolution brought in from outside.

And the moment one way of counting is fixed, a ceiling on the readout appears for the first time: the number of waves distinguishable at that resolution is bounded.

Even more interestingly, running the system for a long time, the number of waves readable in the steady state does not even reach that ceiling. It stabilized at ten to forty percent of the ceiling, because the frequencies condense toward the edge of the band.

## Three dimensions, and particles, were properties of the readout

Reading the four papers together, one picture emerges.

Directions stop at three not because there are only three internal relations. The relations keep multiplying explosively. What saturates is the readout.

Many particle-like units are born not because particles were added from outside. A single wave is unstable and splits on its own.

And the waves appear countable in finite numbers not because the number of waves is a quantity of reality. The moment the counter fixes a resolution, a finite readout comes into being.

The previous article asked: if we add more waves, do we get more spatial dimensions? The current answer reads:

Only the internal relations increase. The readable directions saturate at three; the waves multiply on their own by splitting; and their number is decided by the resolution of whoever reads them.

## What can and cannot be claimed as discovered

Let me draw the boundary honestly.

What is shown here consists of theorems and numerical experiments inside one mathematical model. There is as yet no evidence that this explains why real space is three-dimensional, or any property of real elementary particles.

Still: in a single system that starts from wave relations alone, with no background space assumed, a space-like readout that saturates at three directions, a spontaneous multiplication of particle-like units, and the emergence of finite counting all appear at once, from one and the same mechanism — the saturation of the readout. That one concrete construction with these properties exists is, I believe, what has been shown.
