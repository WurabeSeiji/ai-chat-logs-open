# Do Symmetries Carry Dimensionless Numbers Rather Than Conserved Quantities? - A Catalogue of Symmetries and Magic Numbers for Reading Out Spacetime from a State Space

In "Why Don't the Three 137s Agree Exactly? - Search for Pairs That Are Close but Never Lock: the Fourteenth Thought Experiment", I asked why general relativity and the Standard Model have so many symmetries.

Yet until now I had never put the symmetries themselves in order.

From the start, my aim has been this: without placing a background (spacetime), to read out spacetime and particles from a state space (a set of complex numbers) alone, and to connect that to the fact that the dynamics (general relativity and the Standard Model) hold with high precision.

I have tried many approaches, but I still cannot draw a satisfactory map that reads out spacetime and particles from the state space (the set of complex numbers).

I had fallen into wandering again, so I went back and reorganized everything from the axioms and the symmetries.

Along the way, I noticed that if I take the square z′ = z² of a complex number z as a basic quantity, z′ is itself just a complex number.

The zero closure of the sum of squares, Σzₙ² = 0, which I had treated as an important definition, is exactly the zero closure of the linear sum of z′, Σz′ₙ = 0.

In other words, the zero closure of the sum of squares and the zero closure of the linear sum were the same definition, read at different layers.

I have published the result on Zenodo as a catalogue of symmetries and magic numbers.

Its contents are a rather dry list of definitions, so here I introduce only five observations that should be easy to follow for general readers.

## 1. The two closures were one definition

The starting point of my system is only a set of complex numbers {zₙ}.

I place no xyz space, no time t and no distance at the start.

The elements carry no name tags, and there is no special origin and no special direction.

I call this anonymity, and make it the zeroth axiom.

Since there is no standard outside, the system has no choice but to take itself as its standard.

I call this self-consistency, and make it the first axiom.

To these I added one more axiom.

A readout cannot tell which layer a value it reads belongs to.

Both z and its square z′ = z² are just complex numbers.

From the reading side, there is no way to tell which is the original value and which is the squared one.

This is anonymity applied not to the elements but to the layers.

Under this axiom, the single definition "the sum is 0" has to hold whichever layer it is read at.

As a result, both the zero closure of the linear sum, Σzₙ = 0, and the zero closure of the sum of squares, Σzₙ² = 0, come out.

What looked like two conditions was one definition seen in two readings.

A picture makes this easy to see.

When points are placed on the complex plane, both closures holding means that

the centroid of the points is at the origin,

the spread along the two axes is equal,

and there is no correlation between the two axes.

Anonymity becomes a picture as it stands.

## 2. Space is three-dimensional and continuous; spacetime is four-dimensional and discrete

Next: why 3 + 1 dimensions?

Only in spaces of odd dimension do waves travel without leaving a tail.

This property is called Huygens' principle.

In three dimensions, sound and light arrive at the moment they were emitted, in the shape they were emitted.

In two dimensions this does not happen.

Even after the wave has passed, a lingering tail remains.

Thanks to this property, three-dimensional space can form self-consistent states while staying continuous.

Once time is included, however, the story changes.

As time goes on, the phase rotates.

For self-consistency to keep closing, the rotation of the phase must return exactly to where it started.

With continuous time and a general angle, the phase never returns to the same place and keeps drifting.

To keep closing, the rotation angle must be a rational ratio, and time becomes discrete.

Moreover, for translations in space and rotations of phase to coexist in one structure, a lattice is needed.

The only rotations compatible with a lattice are 1-fold, 2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold and 6-fold symmetry.

This is a well-known fact called the crystallographic restriction.

The lattice on which 4-fold and 6-fold symmetry coexist is the four-dimensional Hurwitz lattice (24 units).

Space is three-dimensional and continuous; spacetime is four-dimensional and discrete.

The two come out as different faces of one and the same mechanism.

## 3. The same conserved quantity goes by different names depending on the side it is seen from

Physics has many examples where there are two sides, the same conserved quantity sits on both, and yet each side calls it by a different name.

In Kaluza-Klein theory, momentum along the fifth axis is called electric charge.

In E = mc², the same thing is called energy on one side and mass on the other.

In my system too, the spatial clock t and the internal clock R are one rotation split into two.

On one side it is called speed, and on the other side mass.

Now recall anonymity.

Under anonymity, the two sides carry no name tags either.

Which side we read from is only a choice of name tags.

Then what looked like separate symmetries and separate conserved quantities will in many cases be different views of one symmetry.

The CPT theorem, which says physics does not change when the sign of charge, left and right, and past and future are swapped all at once, can also be read as a matter of how we choose the tags: which to call positive, which left, which past.

In the catalogue I list seven groups that may merge into one, as candidates.

## 4. Do symmetries carry dimensionless numbers rather than conserved quantities?

In high school I learned Noether's theorem: a symmetry comes with a conserved quantity.

If the laws do not change when time is shifted, energy is conserved; if they do not change when position is shifted, momentum is conserved; if they do not change under rotation, angular momentum is conserved.

Indeed, the symmetries we learned in high school always came paired with a conserved quantity, so, as with dimensional analysis, I found it handy that formulas could be built without memorizing them.

But when I tried the derivation in an earlier article, "I Was Chasing Noether's Theorem, and 'Ordinary Physics' Came Out of Discrete Waves", it needed a far more involved derivation than I had expected.

I began to wonder whether it really always holds, or whether it rests on some assumption.

In the catalogue I list nine places where Noether's theorem fails or is doubtful.

Discrete symmetries have no continuous flow; in gravity, energy cannot be localized; and in quantum anomalies, something conserved classically is broken by quantization.

This time I step a little further into that question, and consider, as my own hypothesis, that wherever there is a symmetry there is at least one dimensionless magic number.

The reason is simple. If I regard my whole system as a state space made of a set of complex numbers, and impose zero closure and self-consistency, every quantity is fixed only as a ratio (an angle), and the theory becomes scale invariant.

Multiply all the complex numbers by the same number, and the zero-closure equation still holds; the unit of the ruler is not fixed.

If so, then perhaps the values that fix structures and laws are not values carrying dimensions (units), but dimensionless numbers. That is the question.

In a world of ratios only, however we exchange the ruler, what survives is just the pure numbers fixed by the structure.

On this view, a conserved quantity is nothing more than that number read out multiplied by a ruler.

There are many real examples.

Atomic nuclei have magic numbers, 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126: when protons or neutrons gather in exactly these numbers, the nucleus is especially stable.

These numbers come from the symmetry of the three-dimensional harmonic oscillator, with a spin-orbit term added.

On this view, the three 137s of the previous article connect as well.

When different symmetries call for nearby numbers, the observed value does not settle on any one of them, and instead is pulled in and wavers between them.

The constants we observe are close to the numbers of the structure, but do not match them.

Eddington once claimed that the inverse of the fine-structure constant is the integer 137, and demanded an exact match.

Mainstream physics, on the grounds of the mismatch, threw away the structural numbers themselves.

I take neither side, and treat the mismatch not as an error but as content to be read.

The world is uncertain and discrete; why, then, does it look continuous and deterministic?

Because the structural numbers provide points to be pulled into, jumps are observed in steps.

Because several numbers compete, the observed value shifts continuously with scale.

Wavering that is not pulled in takes an extremely long time to break down, and in the meantime it looks like a fixed orbit.

## 5. What can be read depends on how we project

Last is the root cause of my wandering.

The same complex number looks entirely different depending on how we project it.

Seen by its size (norm), it is always positive and never 0, and appears to map onto every value.

Rotate it by an angle θ and look along the original direction, and only cos θ remains.

Multiply it by i and look along the original direction, and it becomes 0.

Everything maps, part maps, nothing maps.

These three are not a contradiction; they are differences of projection.

A map from the state space {zₙ} to spacetime amounts to viewing the state space through some projection.

Until the way of projecting is chosen, what can be read and what cannot is not decided.

This is the root cause of why the map between the state space and spacetime is hard.

Even so, there is something we can say.

Since spacetime can be read fairly stably, that projection must be at least a partial projection.

If nothing maps, spacetime does not appear.

If everything maps, neither an unreadable interior, nor uncertainty, nor discreteness remains.

If part maps, the mapped part becomes the readable spacetime, the part that escapes without mapping becomes the unreadable interior and the phase, and uncertainty and discreteness come from that escaped part.

I still cannot draw the map itself.

But where the difficulty lies has become clear.

## The catalogue as a whole

The catalogue lists, in a single format, the symmetries the research programme must satisfy and the dimensionless numbers attached to them, including those not introduced here.

Each entry carries a status (established, theory, candidate, open) and its provenance.

It also includes the symmetries required by the Standard Model and general relativity, the symmetries required by E₈, the four systems of magic numbers (integer, coupling constant, irrational, derived), and pairs of numbers that are close but do not match.

I also mapped every one of the 156 papers I have published so far to the entries.

In the process I renumbered the axioms, and revised claims in earlier papers that α matches exactly into "close but not matching".

Disagreements among numbers are not forced into a single answer; they are kept as open problems that admit several interpretations.

To write that I do not know what I do not know is, for now, the answer.
