[Why Did Two Sharp Valleys Appear Near α⁻¹ = 137 and 128?]
See the comments for details.

We studied two numerical experiments: one in which a wave packet appeared to collapse, and another involving white-cat, black-cat, and gray-cat states. Although the two experiments had different purposes and observables, the same region of the exchange coefficient repeatedly appeared in both.

A full-range scan then revealed extremely sharp responses near regions associated with the inverse fine-structure constant α⁻¹≈137 and the high-energy value around 128. Their positions barely moved when we changed the initial waveform, harmonics, phases, or amplitudes.

We have now identified the cause.

The sharp valleys were finite-order recurrences: the scattering operator returned exactly to the identity after a finite number of iterations. If one exchange-scattering step is denoted by U, then the roots satisfy Uⁿ=I.

This condition belongs to the operator itself, not to the initial state. That is why the response positions remained fixed across different initial conditions and experimental models, as long as they shared the same exchange kernel.

We were looking for α as a scattering-cross-section parameter. What we directly found, however, was not α itself but finite-order recurrence independent of the initial state.

We still do not know why these exact roots lie near α⁻¹≈137 and α⁻¹≈128.946. Is it only a numerical coincidence, or do they share an underlying geometric, algebraic, or physical structure? That is the next research question.
