In the previous report, I showed that the sharp valleys near α⁻¹≈137 and 128 were caused by “finite-order recurrence”: the scattering operator returned exactly to its initial state after a finite number of iterations.

If one exchange-scattering operation is denoted by U, then an exact root satisfies Uⁿ=I. Organizing the root positions further showed that they can be expressed using only two integers, n and m, and π:

Rₙ,ₘ=cos²(πm/n).

We did not enter a free phase angle and use it to construct cos². The requirement that the operator return to its starting point after a finite number of iterations selected a discrete phase, and cos²/sin² emerged as its readout weights.

Tracing this structure further back leads to the first axiom of my paper series, Σxₙ²=0. Requiring a nontrivial solution makes closure impossible within the real numbers alone. In the minimal two-component system, it requires y=±ix—an imaginary direction.

The structure is:

nontrivial quadratic closure → imaginary direction → phase rotation → finite recurrence → a temporal cos²/sin² wave.

If an observer cannot read the internal phase directly and observes only A or B along the time axis, this deterministic time wave cannot be distinguished from a Born-type probability wave. Time evolution creates the cos²/sin² weights, and observation reads them as probabilities.

This is also the same mapping used in my earlier double-slit model. A cos²-shaped fluctuation in the source position was pushed forward to the position of a sharp interference peak. Tallying the peak position from one trial to the next made it appear as a probability wave. In that earlier study, cos² was supplied as an input. The present finite-recurrence structure provides its upstream origin.

This does not prove the entire Born rule of standard quantum mechanics as a single theorem. It does, however, connect the first axiom to time evolution and observation, providing a structure that explains where the two-state cos²/sin² form comes from and why it is observed as a probability wave.
