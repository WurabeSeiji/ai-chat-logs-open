# Could a cos² Wave Oscillating in Time Look Like a Probability Wave When Sampled at Random?

Japanese version:<br>
https://note.com/kiharanoriaki/n/n427655620ae0

In the previous article, I introduced two extremely sharp valleys that appeared near the inverse fine-structure-constant values α⁻¹ ≈ 137 and 128.

Previous article:<br>
https://note.com/kiharanoriaki/n/n2ebe434754e2

One numerical experiment produced behavior resembling wave-packet collapse. The other was the white-cat, black-cat, and gray-cat experiment.

Although the two experiments had different purposes and observables, the same region of the exchange coefficient appeared in both. Even when the initial waveforms and harmonic conditions were changed, the positions of the sharp responses barely moved.

Detailed investigation showed that their cause was not α itself as a scattering cross section. It was “finite-order recurrence”: the scattering operator returned exactly to its initial state after a finite number of iterations.

If one exchange-scattering operation is denoted by U, then at a recurrent root,

Uⁿ = I.

In other words, after n steps, the entire operator completes one exact cycle and returns to its starting point.

This explained why the valleys appeared at the same positions across different initial conditions and different experiments.

But another mystery remained.

Why do these finite-order roots lie near the physically famous values α⁻¹ ≈ 137 and 128?

That question remains unsolved.

While investigating it, however, I noticed another important structure.

## cos² Emerged from Only Two Integers

When the exchange weights that return exactly after a finite number of iterations were organized, every recurrent root could be expressed using two integers, n and m.

Their form is

Rₙ,ₘ = cos²(πm/n).

No freely chosen phase angle had been entered into the experiment as a search condition.

The input was the exchange coefficient R. We swept R over a broad range, and the points that closed after a finite number of iterations appeared as sharp valleys. Only afterward did the analysis show that the root positions were determined by the integer ratio m/n and π, and that they had the form cos².

In other words, we did not begin with a phase angle and use it to construct cos².

The condition of finite recurrence selected the phase as the discrete value

πm/n,

and its readout weight became cos².

## Why Did cos² Matter?

In quantum mechanics, the observation probabilities of a two-state system are expressed in the form cos² and sin².

This is one of the most basic forms of the Born rule.

I had long wondered why probabilities take the form cos².

Standard quantum mechanics, however, begins with complex-valued wavefunctions, complex conjugation, and absolute squares.

If a complex amplitude is multiplied by its conjugate and a phase direction is read as a squared magnitude, the cos² and sin² weights of a two-state system are, in a sense, already fixed by the geometry.

For that reason, it seemed difficult to trace the origin of cos² any further back. I had thought that the Born rule might simply have to be placed as an axiom.

In the present experiment, however, cos² appeared from finite-order roots before we attempted to reproduce the Born rule and without entering a free phase angle.

I therefore investigated where this cos² structure came from.

## The Starting Point Was Σxₙ² = 0

My paper series begins with the basic closure condition

Σxₙ² = 0.

It states that the squared registrations of the components forming a system close nontrivially to zero using only components internal to that system.

If every xₙ is real, then every square is nonnegative. Their sum can equal zero only when every component is zero.

The closure condition used here, however, requires a nontrivial solution in which not all components vanish.

It therefore cannot close within the real numbers alone.

In the minimal two-component case,

x² + y² = 0.

If x is nonzero, this condition requires

y = ±ix.

The imaginary direction is therefore not merely a convenient mathematical tool assumed at the outset.

It emerges as a direction required for a nontrivial sum of squares to close to zero.

From there, the imaginary direction as a quarter-turn, continuous phase rotation, and phases that close after a finite number of iterations connect to the integer ratio m/n and the cos² sequence found above.

The derivational sequence is

nontrivial quadratic closure

→ imaginary direction

→ phase rotation

→ finite recurrence

→ cos² and sin² readout weights.

Without placing conjugate complex numbers at the initial starting point, the first axiom Σxₙ² = 0 leads to the Born-type cos²/sin² structure.

## cos² Appeared Along the Time Direction

An even more important point is that these cos² values are not merely a list of parameters.

When exchange scattering at a finite-order root is iterated along the time direction, the readout weights on the A and B sides vary as cos² and sin² across the time steps.

When A becomes stronger, B becomes weaker. When B becomes stronger, A becomes weaker. After a finite number of steps, the system returns exactly to its initial state.

The cos²/sin² curves were therefore not constructed by supplying an external spatial angle.

They are waves drawn along the direction of time by a finitely recurrent operator.

## Can a Time Wave and a Probability Wave Be Distinguished?

This is where the connection to the Born rule appears.

Suppose an observer cannot see the internal phase of the finite recurrence itself and repeatedly observes only whether the system is in A or B along the time axis.

The observed A/B weights then take the form cos² and sin².

This is the same observable distribution as the two-state probability wave obtained from the Born rule in standard quantum mechanics.

One description is a deterministic time wave undergoing finite recurrence.

The other is interpreted as an intrinsically probabilistic wave.

As long as the observer sees only the A/B outcomes, however, the two cannot be distinguished.

Distinguishing them would require direct access to the underlying finite-recurrence phase, the integer-defined period n and winding number m, or the recurrence itself.

If those structures cannot be observed, the finitely recurrent time wave appears directly as a cos²/sin² probability wave.

This study therefore interprets a Born-type probability wave as

the observational image of a finitely recurrent temporal structure.

In this interpretation, probability is not what creates the cos²/sin² shape. The shape is created by finitely recurrent time evolution.

When the observer does not read the internal phase directly and sees only the A/B outcome, the time wave appears as a probability wave.

The Born rule and the measurement problem are therefore not separate problems. They are two aspects of the same structure. Time evolution creates the cos²/sin² weights, and observation reads those weights as probabilities.

In this sense, the Born rule can be interpreted as the statistical aspect of the measurement problem that appears when a finitely recurrent internal phase is read through an observational cross section.

## Does This Derive the Born Rule?

This study does not claim to have proved the entire Born rule of standard quantum mechanics.

It does not derive arbitrary-dimensional quantum states, every possible measurement operator, post-measurement state change, and environmental interaction as a single theorem.

It does, however, provide a structural explanation for one of the central features of the Born rule:

Why do the probability weights of a two-state system take the form cos² and sin²?

In this model, cos²/sin² are not supplied externally as a probability axiom.

An imaginary direction emerges from the nontrivial quadratic closure Σxₙ² = 0. Its phase advances in time and closes after a finite number of iterations, producing cos²/sin² readout weights.

When an observer who cannot see the internal phase reads this time wave, it is indistinguishable from a Born-type probability wave.

The central result obtained here is therefore:

A cos²/sin² time evolution observationally indistinguishable from a Born probability wave was constructed from nontrivial quadratic closure.

## Why the Result Was Published as Two Papers

I published this result as two separate papers.

The first paper analyzes the condition under which an exchange-symmetric two-channel operator closes exactly after a finite number of iterations and derives the discrete cos²/sin² weights.

This theorem stands independently within standard operator theory.

The second paper goes one step further back. Starting from the first axiom Σxₙ² = 0, it derives why the real numbers alone are insufficient and why an imaginary direction and complex phase are required.

Combining both arguments in one paper from the outset would blur the distinction between the finite-order theorem itself and the foundational meaning of the closure condition.

I therefore first published the independent derivation of cos² from finite-order exchange and then published a second paper tracing the origin of the complex phase back to Σxₙ² = 0.

Reading the two papers in order reveals the full sequence:

Σxₙ² = 0

→ complex phase

→ finite-order recurrence

→ temporal cos²/sin²

→ observation as a Born-type probability wave.

## What Remains Unresolved?

Two major questions remain.

The first is why the finite-order roots lie near the physical values α⁻¹ ≈ 137 and 128.

This is still unknown. Whether the agreement is only a numerical coincidence or reflects a shared geometric, algebraic, or physical structure remains an open problem.

The second is whether the integer periods and internal phases of the finite recurrence can be identified directly in a real experiment.

An A/B probability distribution alone cannot distinguish this model from the standard Born rule. To develop it into a physically distinguishable theory, we must search for additional observables specific to the finite-recurrence structure.

Observational indistinguishability does not make the result meaningless.

The discovery is that the same observational image can be constructed from an underlying temporal structure instead of placing the probability wave as an initial probability axiom.
