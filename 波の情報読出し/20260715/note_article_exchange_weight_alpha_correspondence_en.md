# Why Did 137 Appear While Studying Wave-Packet Collapse?

I have published a new paper.

The theme this time is a little strange.

I was not originally trying to study the fine-structure constant.

I was looking more closely at questions that came from the wave-packet localization experiment and the white-cat, black-cat, and gray-cat experiment.

Why does a broad wave become more localized when it interacts with a localized wave?

At what interaction strength does a gray-cat-like mixed state become easiest to form?

I tested these questions in a closed wave system that does not assume even background spacetime in advance.

But when I scanned the interaction strength, a famous number from theoretical physics appeared.

That number was about 137, the inverse of the fine-structure constant.

Even more interestingly, another candidate appeared near the high-energy value where the inverse of the fine-structure constant is around 128.

Of course, this paper does not claim to have fully derived the fine-structure constant.

But a very simple closed exchange map produced candidate regions close to two important numerical regions in standard theory.

I think this is at least worth investigating carefully.

## What Is 137?

In physics, there is an important constant that describes the strength of electromagnetism.

It is called the fine-structure constant.

This constant is about one part in 137.

So its inverse is about 137.

This number is famous in physics.

Why this value?

Why not 136 or 138?

That is still a deep question.

The value is also not completely fixed across all energy scales.

At high energy, the electromagnetic interaction becomes slightly stronger, and the inverse of the fine-structure constant moves toward about 128.

So, roughly speaking, it is about 137 at low energy.

At high energy, it is about 128.

Both numbers are very important in standard theory.

## I Was Not Directly Studying Alpha

In this experiment, I did not put alpha into the model first.

The question was simpler.

There are two waves, A and B.

When they interact, how much of the state moves to the other side?

I called this the exchange weight.

In the paper, I use R as the interaction knob.

If R is large, more of the state remains on its original side.

If one minus R is large, more of the state is exchanged to the other side.

The main object of this paper is not R itself.

It is how strongly the state exchange is established.

## The First Clue Came From Wave-Packet Localization

In an earlier experiment, I let a broad wave interact with a localized wave.

A was a broad, low-order wave.

B was a sharply localized wave.

When these two waves interacted repeatedly through fermion-like intermediate scattering, localization moved into the A side as well.

That was an interesting result for thinking about wave-packet collapse or localization.

But there was one value that kept standing out.

It was R around 0.70.

Why did that region work so well?

That became the first question.

## The Second Clue Came From the Gray-Cat Experiment

Next, I studied the white-cat, black-cat, and gray-cat system.

These are not real cats.

They are names for two allocation states inside a closed wave system.

If A dominates, I call it a white cat.

If B dominates, I call it a black cat.

If A and B are mixed, I call it a gray cat.

In that experiment, I tested which interaction strength makes a gray-cat-like metastable state easiest to form.

Again, the region near R around 0.70 looked important.

So a similar value appeared both in localization exchange and in the gray-cat metastable boundary.

Was this just a coincidence?

Or does the closed exchange map itself select a natural exchange weight?

That is the question of this paper.

## One Extra Harmonic Was Enough

First, I changed the harmonic structure in the localization exchange experiment.

Here, harmonics are additional vibration components that can sharpen the waveform or make it more localized.

The result was surprising.

The harmonic did not have to be odd.

Even harmonics also worked.

Mixed odd and even harmonics also worked.

The phase could drift.

The wavelength could drift.

The harmonic amplitude could become extremely small.

But when the extra harmonic was removed completely, the sharp candidate disappeared.

So the precise shape did not seem to be the main point.

What mattered was whether at least one additional degree of freedom existed.

This is important.

It suggests that the selected exchange weight is not simply caused by carefully tuning the waveform.

The presence of an additional vibration degree of freedom itself may be involved.

## A Wide Scan Found Two Deep Candidate Regions

Next, I used the gray-cat metastable boundary and scanned R over a wide range.

Several candidate bands appeared.

The figure below shows the depth of those candidate bands.

The deepest candidate appeared near the region corresponding to the low-energy fine-structure constant.

That is the region where the inverse of alpha is about 137.

The second deepest candidate appeared near the region where the inverse of alpha is about 129.4.

This is close to the high-energy region where standard theory gives a value near 128.

I am not saying these are exactly the same values.

But considering the effective precision of the numerical experiment and the limited significant digits of the high-energy reference value, it is not easy to dismiss this as meaningless.

## This Is Not “Putting in 137 and Getting 137 Out”

This point is important.

The experiment did not simply put in 137 and then search around it.

I scanned a wide range.

Then I looked for regions where exchange was especially well established.

The deepest region appeared near the low-energy 137 value.

The second deepest region appeared near the high-energy 128 region.

Of course, this alone is not a full derivation of the fine-structure constant.

But it suggests that a closed exchange map may select its own exchange weight, and that this selected weight may be related to the electromagnetic coupling observed in physics.

## How I Read the Result

My current reading is this.

The main subject of this paper is not alpha itself.

The main subject is state exchange.

Inside a closed wave system, how much of a state moves to the other side?

The exchange weight seems to gather around particular values.

When those values are compared with the electromagnetic coupling used in standard theory, the deepest one is very close to the low-energy 137 region.

Another candidate appears near the high-energy 128 region.

So it is more accurate to say this:

The experiment did not explain 137 completely.

Rather, a closed exchange map produced an intrinsic exchange weight that appears to correspond to 137.

That difference matters.

The first statement would be too strong.

The second statement is what the numerical experiment actually shows.

## What This Does Not Claim

To avoid misunderstanding, I want to be clear about the scope.

This paper does not fully derive the fine-structure constant from first principles.

It does not replace standard theory.

It does not directly compute real electron scattering.

It also does not claim that the high-energy candidate is exactly the same as the alpha value measured around the Z-boson mass scale.

The claim is more limited.

In a closed complex-phase exchange map, when the degree of state exchange is scanned, the deepest candidate band appears near the low-energy fine-structure constant.

A second candidate band appears near the high-energy effective coupling region.

That is the scope of this numerical result.

## Summary

I was studying wave-packet localization.

I was also studying the metastable state of white cats, black cats, and gray cats.

During that work, the interaction strength near R around 0.70 kept appearing.

So I tested which exchange weight a closed exchange map selects.

As a result, the deepest candidate appeared near the low-energy fine-structure constant, where the inverse of alpha is about 137.

A second candidate appeared near the high-energy region, where the inverse of alpha is around 128.

This is not a final conclusion.

But it opens an interesting path.

Inside a closed wave system, a state-exchange weight may be selected spontaneously.

And that selected weight may be connected to the electromagnetic coupling observed in real physics.
